# Blocks matter less than everything around them

Scratch's success does not come mainly from its blocks. Its designers and the evidence point to four other things. The first is an **always-live, fail-soft loop**: you click any block and see it act, you can edit while the program runs, there are no error messages, and state stays on screen. The second is a **small, curated, game-level vocabulary** written as plain-language sentences. The third is **personal media**: your own drawings and your own voice. The fourth is a **community where every shared project can be opened and remixed**. The blocks themselves give a real but modest and front-loaded advantage. In the best controlled study, blocks students led by **d ≈ 0.58 after five weeks**, and the lead **disappeared entirely after ten weeks of Java**. Meta-analyses comparing blocks with text find small effects that are often not significant. Godot removed its visual scripting because **only 0.5% of more than 5,000 users** used it as their main language. Its own post-mortem blamed the missing high-level game vocabulary, not the notation. For Morgan's engine, the design doc already holds the parts the evidence supports best: text as the source of truth, a dual view and diffable files. The open risks are on the other side. They include liveness under determinism, Python's loud exceptions next to Scratch's silent defaults, a palette generated by reflection that may grow too large and too low-level, and the way ECS spreads one entity's behavior across many queries, where Scratch keeps "the entire story" in one sprite. This report separates what the sources found from what the researchers extrapolated. It frames each engine-specific point as an option or a question, not a decision.

**How to read the labels.** **[Source]** marks a finding reported by a cited primary or secondary source. **[Extrapolation]** marks a conclusion the researchers drew for this engine. No source studied a block editor over a deterministic, replay-hashed ECS, so every engine-specific mechanism below is an extrapolation.

## The blocks advantage is real, modest, and gone by week fifteen

The cleanest comparison is Weintrop and Wilensky's. They used the same school, the same teacher, the same curriculum and the same environment (Pencil.cc), and changed only the modality, with 60 students. **[Source]** After five weeks, blocks students scored **66.6% against 58.8%** for text students (d = 0.58). The largest gap was on comprehension of whole programs (d = 0.86). There was **no difference in enjoyment**. Blocks students reported more interest in further CS (8.59 against 7.18 out of 10), and they also rated programming as *harder*. The authors read this as a sign that the students did not count their block work as "programming" ([Weintrop & Wilensky 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf)). After ten more weeks in Java, both groups scored **64.9% and 64.7%** (p = .99). Their javac error rates were the same (53.2% against 53.7%). "All of the students started and ended in the same place… but the two conditions took different paths" ([Weintrop & Wilensky 2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop&Wilensky_2019_C&E.pdf)). A meta-analysis of 13 blocks-versus-text studies found g = 0.245 for cognitive outcomes and g = 0.195 for affective outcomes, **neither significant** under random effects ([Xu et al. 2019](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1218045)). The larger pooled effects in other meta-analyses, such as g = .473 ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958826004434)), compare blocks with instruction in general, not with text.

The robust and mechanical benefit is **less time lost to syntax**. In a controlled study, middle-school students using Stride, a frame-based editor between blocks and text, completed more objectives and spent significantly less time on syntax edits and with code that would not compile ([Price et al. 2016](https://isnap.csc.ncsu.edu/home/public/publication/price-2016-icer)). The best-supported design is the **dual view**. In the largest study found, a CS1 course used a dual-modality editor (n = 425) and was compared with a text-only semester (n = 248). The dual-modality group scored higher on code reading and definitional exam questions, but **not on the SCS1 concept inventory** ([Blanchard et al. 2020](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/775/2020/01/blanchard-et-al-SIGCSE2020.pdf)). Pencil Code logs show learners drifting from blocks to text over a course and **returning to blocks when new constructs appeared** ([Weintrop & Holbert 2017](https://ccl.netlogo.org/2017/Blocks.pdf)). The blocks most often added after a switch back from text were movement (30.5%), art (21.6%) and control (18.0%). That suggests learners use the palette to find the API ([Holbert & Weintrop 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/HolbertWeintrop.pdf)).

**[Extrapolation]** The evidence supports blocks as an on-ramp and a way to discover the API, not as lasting superiority. The design doc's "lossless view of Python" is the evidence-favored architecture. The claim it can make honestly is "you get started faster and never hit a wall", not "you learn more". Because learners switch back and forth one construct at a time, the per-function lock in design doc 4.1 matters. If learners can switch per function, the editor matches what learners actually do. If switching is one-way, it does not. Whether to switch per function or per file is Morgan's call.

## Tinkerability is a property of the whole loop, not the notation

Resnick and Rosenbaum name three principles: **"immediate feedback, fluid experimentation, and open exploration"** ([Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)). Each is built from specific features. **[Source]** You can click a block "while it is still in the palette". There are "no separate steps for compiling, no separate modes for editing", and you can change a `move` number while the script runs. Blocks start "with reasonable default values for their inputs", so a click does something visible. Loose stacks may lie around the workspace, unlike environments "where you need to keep your workspace tidy" (same source). Scratch's language paper says it is "always live", and that removing "jarring mode switches and compilation pauses" keeps users engaged ([Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)). Victor's critique sharpens this: "live coding, as a standalone feature, misses the point". The environment must also show the flow and the state, and "All state must be eliminated or shown" ([Victor, Learnable Programming](https://worrydream.com/LearnableProgramming/)).

The details matter. **[Source]** In scratch-vm, the glow is computed every frame from thread state, covers the whole stack, and appears only for the sprite open in the editor. Clicking a running stack stops it. Monitors are re-evaluated every step and pushed to the UI only when they change ([scratch-vm runtime.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scratchfoundation/scratch-vm/develop/src/engine/runtime.js)). At first, new variables were not shown on the stage, and "Many users did not discover variables". After the team showed them by default, "many more users started using variables" ([Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)).

**Fail-soft is designed into each primitive, not handled by a global exception handler.** **[Source]** "Scratch has no error messages", and "every block attempts to do something sensible even when presented with out-of-range inputs", because "a program that runs, even if it is not correct, feels closer to working than a program that does not run." The authors concede that "eliminating error messages does not eliminate errors" ([Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)). In code, scratch-vm turns NaN into 0, compares strings case-insensitively, makes invalid list indexes no-ops, and treats `''`, `'0'` and `'false'` as false. Its `random` list index calls the unseeded `Math.random` ([cast.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scratchfoundation/scratch-vm/develop/src/util/cast.js)). "Create clone" silently does nothing past 300 clones ([runtime.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scratchfoundation/scratch-vm/develop/src/engine/runtime.js)). Fraser adds that blocks still have one invisible syntax error, a block that sits *near* a stack without being attached to it. Blockly fixes this by bumping such blocks apart ([Fraser 2015](https://docs.blockly.com/publications/papers/TenThingsWeveLearnedFromBlockly.pdf)).

**Scratch is not deterministic in the sense this engine requires.** **[Source]** Each frame the sequencer runs passes over its threads until 75% of the frame budget is spent (`WORK_TIME`). Warp loops run for up to 500 ms of wall-clock time. Script order follows sprite *layer* order, so bringing a sprite to the front changes what its scripts do ([sequencer.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scratchfoundation/scratch-vm/develop/src/engine/sequencer.js); [runtime.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scratchfoundation/scratch-vm/develop/src/engine/runtime.js)). Within one pass, threads yield only at waits and at the ends of loops, never between an `if` test and its body, so users can "reason about a script in isolation" ([Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)). Classroom studies found that students struggled with "repeated execution, variables, and concurrency" ([Meerbaum-Salant et al. 2013](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/learning-computer-science-concepts-with-scratch/)).

**[Extrapolation]** The researchers see the engine's determinism as both a constraint and an opportunity. As a constraint, every Scratch liveness feature that changes the world has to become *input*. A click on a block, or an edit while the game runs, would be recorded and applied at a tick boundary so replays reproduce it. Alternatively it could run in a throwaway fork made from the existing snapshot and restore. As an opportunity, deterministic replay allows things Scratch never had: stepping backward, scrubbing time (Victor's request), and a defined order (StableId, pipeline phase) that does not depend on render layers. The design doc already lists glow, step, pause and rewind (10.7 item 5), and leaves open how a stray stack gets its parameters (4.1). The research adds questions inside those items. Should clicking a *reporter*, a pure read shown in a bubble, be allowed everywhere because it cannot affect the hash? Should every reflected block carry an illuminating default, not a type default of 0? Should a new component field appear by default in the inspector or as a monitor? Python raises where Scratch shrugs. The researchers see three options: catch exceptions per handler and mark the failing block red while the world keeps running; give block-facing native primitives a documented clamping contract; or both. The Scratch-style option of hiding errors inside Python helpers would break the "blocks are just Python" round trip. Whatever behavior is chosen has to be deterministic itself.

## Vocabulary and curation beat block mechanics

Godot's post-mortem is the most direct warning for a palette generated from reflection. **[Source]** VisualScript "never gained traction". Beginners "found GDScript easy to learn", and "Godot lacked high level components to make use of it". Godot contrasted itself with Unreal, GameMaker and Construct, which pair visual scripting with packaged, high-level game features. Visual *shaders* "work well" and were kept ([Godot blog](https://godotengine.org/article/godot-4-will-discontinue-visual-scripting)). Scratch's designers treat palette size as a constant fight. **[Source]** "Every command consumes screen space … so there is a higher 'cost' to increasing the command set". Scratch 1.0 had 92 blocks and 1.4 had 125. The mitigations were drop-down consolidation, blocks that "appear on demand" (variable blocks appear after a variable exists), and the most self-explanatory blocks at the top of each category ([Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)). Bau et al. put a typical vocabulary at 100–200 words and warn that "recalling 100–200 concepts can overwhelm a newcomer". They credit palettes because they "persist" and "organize concepts by topic instead of by name" ([Bau et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)).

**Block text is written, not derived.** **[Source]** Students credit the English-like text: "Java is not in English it's in Java language, and the blocks are in English" ([Weintrop 2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop_CACM_2019.pdf)). Resnick and Silverman's "choose black boxes carefully" shows how far curation can go. A three-input RGB `setcolor` became a single 0–100 input so children could write `forever [setcolor temperature]`: "the best way to deal with three dimensions was to throw away two of them" ([Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)). Fraser reports that conditionals and loops confused learners until Blockly "moved conditionals into the logic group, changed the colour, and the problem went away" ([Fraser 2015](https://docs.blockly.com/publications/papers/TenThingsWeveLearnedFromBlockly.pdf)). MakeCode, the closest shipped analogue to a palette generated from declarations, uses comment metadata on its API for block text, hidden optional parameters, defaults and categories ([Ball et al. 2019](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2019/10/splashws19splashemain-p8-p-41a6cf2-42682-final.pdf)).

**Locality is the deepest tension with ECS.** **[Source]** In Scratch, "commands operate only on the sprite in which they appear". An early prototype allowed cross-sprite commands, "but users found that confusing". The payoff is that "the scripts in a given sprite tell the entire story about that sprite's behavior". Broadcasts let sprites "be moved between projects without breaking dependencies" ([Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)). The event-sheet tools offer an alternative that already looks like an ECS. **[Source]** In GDevelop, "events run in the order they are listed from top to bottom" every frame. Conditions decide both when actions happen and which objects they affect ([GDevelop wiki](https://wiki.gdevelop.io/gdevelop5/events/)). In Construct, an object that no condition mentions means "every instance". Picking is the classic pitfall, because sub-events re-filter and can empty the parent's set ([Construct manual](https://www.construct.net/en/animation-software/manual/project-primitives/events/how-events-work); [bugnet.io](https://bugnet.io/blog/how-to-fix-construct3-sub-event-scope-losing-picked-instances)).

**[Extrapolation]** An event-sheet row is nearly a Flecs system: the conditions are query terms, the actions are the body, and sheet order is run order within a phase. Kodu's pages correspond to groups of systems enabled together, which is what a game state is. The design doc already has the hat decide scope, and keeps a component's name visible in the block (4.7). The research raises further questions. If the generated palette follows design doc 4.7 (get, set and change per field), does it need MakeCode-style annotations for label templates, defaults and hidden parameters, and on-demand visibility, to stay near Scratch's scale? Should "Why does this paddle move?" have a one-click answer, such as an inspector list of the systems and observers matching the selected entity, glowing when they ran? Should a rule's WHEN show the live count of matched entities, to defuse the picking pitfall? Should system order be visible, and perhaps draggable, the way sheet order is? Design doc 4.7 is **[Leaning]**, and these questions sit inside it.

## Meaning, media and remixing drive adoption more than code does

Scratch's designers name three historical failure modes of 1980s Logo and BASIC. **[Source]** The languages were "too difficult to use"; the activities were "not connected to young people's interests"; and "no one could provide guidance when things went wrong". The answers were tinkerable, meaningful and social ([Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)). **Blocks address only the first.** "More meaningful" meant personalization (importing photos, recording voices) and diversity. A concrete decision followed: "we decided to focus on 2D images, rather than 3D, since it is much easier for people to create, import, and personalize 2D artwork" (same source). Resnick and Silverman judge a kit by the *variance* of what people make: "If the creations from a class of students are all similar to one another, we feel that something has gone wrong" ([Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)). Framing has measured effects. Compared with generic Alice, Storytelling Alice gave **42% more programming time**, and students were **three times as likely** to keep working on their projects ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/development/girls-ask-alice-for-programming-skills/)). Guzdial's Media Computation cut CS1 failure and withdrawal from **over 50% to under 15%**, but he rejects the hypothesis that it made students learn more, and offers no proven mechanism ([Guzdial 2013](https://carpentries-i18n.github.io/instructor-training/files/papers/guzdial-mediacomp-retrospective-2013.pdf)).

**The community is half the product.** **[Source]** The website launched together with the language in 2007. Remixes made up "over 15%" of projects in 2009 and "more than a quarter" by 2014 ([Resnick 2014](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/constructionism-2014.pdf)). Across 2.4 million projects, users who remix more have larger command repertoires, even controlling for the number of projects and the amount of code. The authors call the effect "modest" ([Dasgupta et al. 2016](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=168717)). Only about **one in ten** projects is ever remixed. The project attributes that attract remixes also produce *less original* remixes ([Hill & Monroy-Hernández 2013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.01295)). Automatic attribution links are valued less than credit given by a person ([Monroy-Hernández et al. 2011](http://www.media.mit.edu/publications/computers-cant-give-credit-how-automatic-attribution-falls-short-in-an-online-remixing-community/)). Newcomers who comment on other people's projects in their first 14 days have about **2.58 times the odds** of later receiving "loves" ([CSCW 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.04046)). Social design has failure modes too. A LittleBigPlanet trophy for maximum hearts led to heart spamming that made the ratings nearly useless ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/media-molecule-finds-success-and-failure-alike-in-i-little-big-planet-i-s-sharing-systems)). Dreams' community lost its live service when support ended ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/media-molecule-expands-i-dreams-i-content-usage-rules-before-service-shutdown)). Scratch dropped CC BY-SA for projects uploaded after **22 January 2026**, which left their reuse outside the site unclear ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language)); [Scratch Wiki](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Creative_Commons_License)). Cloud variables were limited to numbers and closed to new accounts, which allowed multiplayer games without free-text chat ([Scratch Wiki, Cloud Data](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Cloud_Data)).

**[Extrapolation]** The engine's projects are already plain text (`sage-scene v1` and Python), which suits "see inside" and remixing better than Scratch's archive or Unreal's binary Blueprints, which do not merge ([Epic blog](https://www.unrealengine.com/blog/diffing-unreal-assets?lang=zh-CN)). The design doc lists sharing, remixing, localization and accessibility as "smaller" gaps (10.7 item 10). The research suggests they carry more weight than that label implies. That is a prioritization question for Morgan. It is not a scope change while Milestone 0 is open. Concretely, the research raises these questions. Should a project carry provenance (remixed from) and a human-written credits field? Any such field is a format change that needs Morgan's approval. Should the licence be recorded per project from the start? Should a new project open with a character that already has a sprite and a sound, the way the Scratch cat does, and should a pixel editor sit next to the blocks, as in MakeCode Arcade? Should starter templates ask the user to change and extend them, rather than present puzzles? Resnick: "learning to code by solving logic puzzles is like learning to write by solving crossword puzzles" ([Resnick 2014](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/constructionism-2014.pdf)). Fraser found that students would not read written instructions, and that popups which close only when the student performs the required action were what worked ([Fraser 2015](https://docs.blockly.com/publications/papers/TenThingsWeveLearnedFromBlockly.pdf)). That suggests tutorials built as editor-side observers.

Two caveats temper the table. Points 1–4 have the strongest support, but the support comes from designer rationale and code, not from controlled trials. The only quantitative anecdote is the variable-visibility change. Point 5 is the researchers' biggest concern and has the thinnest evidence: no source evaluates query-based behavior with novices. Several facts also went unverified. ## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "how do we make good blocks?" to "how do we make a good loop and a good vocabulary that blocks can show?" The design doc's bets (text as truth, lossless round trip, diffable files, one runtime) sit where the evidence is strongest. Godot's failure and Scratch's fight over its palette show that the danger lies in what the blocks *say*. A faithful projection of a large, low-level API reproduces VisualScript. Determinism makes the hard part harder, because every liveness feature that changes the world has to pass through recorded input. It also makes things possible that Scratch never could: rewind, time scrubbing and a run order that does not depend on render layers. Those may matter more for learning than the glow itself.

The least obvious finding is that Scratch's comprehensibility rests on *locality*: one sprite's scripts tell its whole story. An ECS deliberately removes that. Game tools that work on ECS-like data (event sheets) succeed by making order and the matched set visible, and they still trip users on picking. Whether the editor can give back a per-entity "story" view without giving up systems as queries is likely to decide whether the low floor holds. No source has tested it. It is the question most worth prototyping and watching real users on, following Resnick's rule to "give people what they want, not what they ask for."
