# Scratch's popularity: community, sharing, remix culture and ecosystem

Research date: 2026-11-14. Scope: the non-technical reasons people adopted Scratch, not block mechanics. Every number has a source and a date. Where a source is secondary (Wikipedia, Scratch Wiki, forums, vendor blogs) the bullet says so. Claims I could not source are listed under Gaps rather than asserted.

Access notes: `scratch.mit.edu/statistics` loads its numbers with JavaScript, so the fetch returned labels with no values. `annualreport.scratchfoundation.org` failed a TLS certificate check (a Netlify misconfiguration), and the 2023 report PDF on Squarespace returned 404. The Scratch Team's Medium blog returned 403. Figures from those sources are therefore taken from search-result excerpts, and are marked as such.

## Usage numbers over time (users, projects, MAU, countries, languages, ages)

### Takeaway
The best-sourced recent figures are about 135 million registered users, about 164 million shared projects and about 109 million monthly visits (official statistics page as cited by Wikipedia, October 2026). The one billionth project created (shared and unshared) came on 12 April 2024. Growth went from 1 million registered members in about five years (2007–2012) to more than 100 million in the 2020s. Shared projects are only a small fraction of created projects: roughly 164 million shared against more than 1 billion created.

### Cited Findings
- Milestones: Scratch 1.0 on 15 May 2007; Scratch 2.0 (online editor in the browser) on 9 May 2013; Scratch 3.0 on 2 January 2019. The one millionth project was on 21 April 2010 and the one billionth on 12 April 2024 — [Wikipedia, Scratch (programming language)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language)) (fetched 2026-11-14)
- As of 20 October 2026 the official site reports more than 164 million projects shared, 135 million registered users and 109 million monthly visits. More than 1.3 billion projects created in total is tagged "citation needed" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))
- The official statistics snapshot from July 2024, as reported in a search excerpt, shows 135,078,559 registered users, 164,307,034 projects shared, 989,937,824 comments and 34,866,300 studios. The users and projects figures matching the October 2026 Wikipedia figures suggests the official page has not been updated since mid-2024 — [Scratch Wiki, Scratch Statistics](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Scratch_Statistics); [Discuss Scratch, "how many users are on scratch in 2025"](https://scratch.mit.edu/discuss/topic/841129/) (forum users estimate about 160 million users and 250 million shared projects; unverified)
- The 2024 annual report, according to search excerpts: the one billionth project, 21.5 million new users in 2024, a "reach of more than 140 million children", 54 new partner organizations in 20 countries, and 800+ Scratch Days (now Scratch Week) events in 30 countries. A Scratch Week figure of 5,000 projects shared was also reported — [Scratch Foundation, 2024 Annual Report (PDF)](https://annualreport.scratchfoundation.org/pdf/Scratch-Report_2024.pdf); [annual report site](https://annualreport.scratchfoundation.org/)
- The same report describes a user study with two segments, "Coding Creators" (complex, original projects) and "Social Creators" (who thrive on community interaction), and says 86% of users enjoy connecting with others (search excerpt; I could not open the full methodology) — [2024 Annual Report](https://annualreport.scratchfoundation.org/pdf/Scratch-Report_2024.pdf)
- When Monroy-Hernández's thesis was written (2012), the community had more than 1 million registered members and 2 million projects after about five years — [DSpace@MIT, *Designing for Remixing*](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/78202)
- Activity, from a third-party blog: about 1.4 million monthly active project creators and about 625,000 monthly active comment creators. The US accounted for 33.05% of accounts as of April 2024. These are secondary figures, probably read off the statistics page — [JetLearn, Scratch statistics](https://www.jetlearn.com/blog/scratch-statistics)
- Languages: translated into 70+ languages. Target ages are given as 5–16 in the infobox, and the community is "designed primarily for ages 8 to 16 but used by all ages" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))
- China blocked the website in August 2020, when more than 3 million people there were estimated to use it (GreatFire report). Wikipedia calls this the only country-level ban — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))
- Revenue: ProPublica's extraction of the Form 990 shows 2024 revenue of $20,013,298, about 99% from contributions. Another summary of 990 data gives 2023 revenue of $8,770,345. Each source covers a different scope — [ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/462612143); [Civic Intelligence 990 summary](https://siviq.co/nonprofits/organizations/462612143/scratch-foundation/returns/202123029349300312/2020)

### Inferences
- The step change in access came with Scratch 2.0 in 2013 (browser editor, no install). Projects passed 1 million in 2010 and 1 billion in 2024; most of that growth came after the move to the browser and after Hour of Code era school adoption. This is a correlation; I found no causal analysis.
- Shared projects (164 million) are small next to created projects (more than 1 billion). Most making is private or classroom work, so the community is a layer on top of a much larger base of solo use.
- The official counter appears to be stale since 2024, so any 2025–2026 user count is an estimate.

### Gaps
- Official monthly active users and the age distribution of new users: the statistics page needs JavaScript, and I could not get its current values. The Wikipedia figures may themselves be copies of a stale counter.
- The 2023 and 2025 annual reports: the 2023 PDF returned 404 and no 2025 report was found.
- Full methodology of the 2024 "Coding Creators / Social Creators" study: the Medium blog posts ("Exploring Engagement and Well-being in the Scratch Community", parts 1 and 2) returned 403.

## The online community: sharing, project pages, "See inside", studios, comments, loves, curators

### Takeaway
Scratch was designed as a website of creators, not just an editor. The community features (share, project page, see inside, remix, studios, comments) were built as one system, with remixing at the centre, by Monroy-Hernández under Resnick. Social engagement is a large part of the motivation: the 2024 annual report says 86% of users enjoy connecting with others.

### Cited Findings
- Monroy-Hernández's MIT doctoral thesis, *Designing for Remixing: Supporting an Online Community of Amateur Creators* (advisor Mitchel Resnick, 176 pages), is built on the Scratch Online Community, which he helped create. It asks three questions: the *process* of remixing (its role in cultural production and social learning), the *conditions* (which attributes affect remixing) and *attitudes* (how people feel about being remixed) — [DSpace@MIT](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/78202); [MIT Media Lab publication page](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/designing-for-remixing-supporting-an-online-community-of-amateur-creators/); [Berkman Center talk, 31 Jan 2012](https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/luncheon/2012/01/monroy-hernandez)
- Studios: Scratchers can submit projects to studios, and curators help manage them. Users can share, remix and join studios. Moderators and Scratch Team accounts are labelled on their profiles (an asterisk after the username) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))
- Scale of the social layer as of July 2024: about 990 million comments and 34.9 million studios, against 164 million shared projects (snapshot via search excerpt) — [Scratch Wiki, Scratch Statistics](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Scratch_Statistics)
- Related work by Monroy-Hernández and Hill: "Cooperation and attribution in an online community of young creators" — [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/cooperation-and-attribution-in-an-online-community-of-young-creators)

### Inferences
- About 6 comments per shared project shows that feedback is the main social currency, more than remixing. About 1 in 10 projects is remixed (next section).
- "Social Creators" being a named segment in the Foundation's own research suggests that a large share of users come for the community, not for programming.

### Gaps
- Design rationale for specific UI elements (the Share button in the editor, "See inside", love/favorite, the front page's "Featured" and "Curated by" rows) from primary sources. My background knowledge says these exist; the thesis text would be the primary source, but its PDF needs permission to reproduce and I did not read it.

## Remixing: mechanics, attribution, and its effect on learning and newcomers

### Takeaway
Remixing is automatic and attributed on Scratch (a "remix" copies the project and links back to the parent, forming remix trees). Three research results stand out. (1) It helps learning: users who remix more know more commands, and exposure to concepts through remixes predicts using them. (2) There is a trade-off: the features that make projects get remixed more also produce less original remixes. (3) Automatic attribution is not enough socially: kids value credit given by a person more than credit generated by the system. A licence change on 22 January 2026 ended CC BY-SA for new projects, which matters for remix culture outside the site.

### Cited Findings
- **Remixing and learning**: Dasgupta, Hale, Monroy-Hernández and Hill, "Remixing as a Pathway to Computational Thinking", CSCW 2016 (DOI 10.1145/2818048.2819984). The data are more than 2.4 million projects by more than 1 million users. Users who remix more have larger repertoires of programming commands even after controlling for the number of projects and the amount of code shared. Exposure to computational thinking concepts through remixing is associated with a higher likelihood of using those concepts — [Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/remixing.pdf); [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/remixing-as-a-pathway-to-computational-thinking); [Community Data Science blog](https://blog.communitydata.science/studying-the-relationship-between-remixing-learning/)
- Measurement caveat from the same authors: Scratch has no curriculum, lesson plan or quizzes, so measuring learning there is an open problem — [Community Data Science blog](https://blog.communitydata.science/studying-the-relationship-between-remixing-learning/)
- **The remixing dilemma**: Hill and Monroy-Hernández, "The Remixing Dilemma: The Trade-Off Between Generativity and Originality", *American Behavioral Scientist* 57(5):643–663, 2013. The data cover every Scratch remix and its parent. Only about one tenth of Scratch projects are ever remixed. The project attributes associated with more remixing are also associated with less original, less transformative derivatives — [Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/hill_monroy-remixing_dilemma.pdf); [arXiv 1507.01295](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1507.01295); [Boing Boing summary, "What makes a project remixable?", May 2013](https://boingboing.net/2013/05/10/what-makes-a-project-remixable.html)
- **Automatic attribution falls short**: Monroy-Hernández, Hill, Gonzalez-Rivero and boyd, "Computers Can't Give Credit: How Automatic Attribution Falls Short in an Online Remixing Community", 2011 (CHI 2011, going by the PDF filename). The study combines a quantitative analysis of a design change that added automatic attribution with 12 semi-structured interviews. Automatic credit, such as a system-generated list of contributors, plays a different role from human-given credit and is valued less — [MIT Media Lab](http://www.media.mit.edu/publications/computers-cant-give-credit-how-automatic-attribution-falls-short-in-an-online-remixing-community/)
- Responses to being remixed have their own study: "Responses to Remixing on a Social Media Sharing Website" (ICWSM) — [AAAI ICWSM](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/14028)
- **Licence change**: Wikipedia's infobox lists CC BY-SA 2.0 for projects uploaded before 22 January 2026 and a proprietary licence after. The Scratch Wiki says the new licence is "much stricter than the Creative Commons license". On the Snap! forum, users read it as forbidding sharing projects outside Scratch. Whether old projects keep CC BY-SA is disputed on the forums; I found no official clarification — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language)); [Scratch Wiki, Creative Commons License](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Creative_Commons_License); [Discuss Scratch, "CC BY-SA 2.0 is gone"](https://scratch.mit.edu/discuss/topic/866892/); [Snap! forum](https://forum.snap.berkeley.edu/t/can-scratch-projects-legally-be-remixed-to-snap/21816); [Scratch Terms of Service](https://mitscratch.freshdesk.com/en/support/solutions/articles/4000219182-scratch-terms-of-service)

### Inferences
- Remixing is a learning onramp: the code is visible ("See inside") and can be copied in one click, so kids learn by modifying working programs. The learning result is correlational, though (selection effects are possible).
- The dilemma means a platform has to choose what to reward. If it rewards remix counts it gets many shallow remixes; if it wants originality it has to reward transformative remixes.
- Attribution needs a human-authored part (a "credits" or "notes" field, thanks in comments), not only a machine-generated parent link.
- The 2026 licence change shows how dependent remix culture is on the platform's terms: the moment terms change, sharing outside the site becomes legally murky.

### Gaps
- Primary evidence on whether remixing *hurts* newcomers (for example plagiarism disputes, "remix spam"); the attribution papers suggest conflict exists, but I did not get numbers.
- Technical details of remix trees (the parent/root fields in project metadata) from a primary source.

## Moderation, safety and community guidelines for a child audience

### Takeaway
Safety constraints shaped features directly. The clearest example is cloud variables: numbers only, so free-text chat is impossible, and unavailable to "New Scratcher" accounts. New accounts also get comment rate limits. Moderators are visible on the site.

### Cited Findings
- New accounts get the "New Scratcher" role. They cannot use cloud data and must wait 30 seconds between comments until they become eligible for "Scratcher" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))
- Cloud variables hold only numbers (letters and symbols are not allowed). Hexadecimal support was removed and a February 2018 Scratch Team post excluded E-notation. Limits: 10 cloud variables per project, 256 digits each (previously 128), and at most 10 updates per second. Cloud chat rooms are forbidden because they are too hard to moderate, except projects with fixed sentences or emoji. Cloud lists have been repeatedly requested and refused. A WebSocket-based cloud system replaced the old one between August and 11 September 2017, after the infrastructure began failing in October 2016 under projects sending up to 30 requests per variable per second — [Scratch Wiki, Cloud Data](https://en.scratch-wiki.info/wiki/Cloud_Data) (community wiki; it cites Scratch Team posts)
- The Scratch Team and moderators are visibly labelled on their profiles — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))
- Forum users report cloud variables were down around February 2026, which broke multiplayer games (user reports, no official status page) — [Discuss Scratch](https://scratch.mit.edu/discuss/topic/839233)

### Inferences
- "Numbers only" is a design pattern: a channel that can carry game state but not free speech. Multiplayer games survive (players encode state as digits) while the cost of moderation stays low.
- Trust tiers (New Scratcher → Scratcher) gate risky features behind a history of good behaviour; this is cheap and effective.

### Gaps
- Moderation staffing, report volumes and the content-filter approach: no primary figures found.
- COPPA/GDPR-K specifics (what is collected from under-13s) were not researched.

## Institutional factors: free, in the browser, schools, Hour of Code, educators, events, translations, ScratchJr, funding

### Takeaway
Scratch is free, has run in the browser since 2013, is owned by a non-profit and is translated into 70+ languages, so a school can adopt it without a budget or IT. Funding is philanthropic: NSF grants early, then Google.org, the LEGO Foundation and others through the Scratch Foundation. Educator communities (ScratchEd at Harvard), a global day of events (Scratch Day, now Scratch Week) and ScratchJr for ages 5–7 widen the funnel at both ends.

### Cited Findings
- The Scratch Foundation (formerly the Code-to-Learn Foundation, founded 2013) owns Scratch and ScratchJr. The Scratch Team moved from the MIT Media Lab to the Foundation on 11 March 2019; the Media Lab still collaborates — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language)); [Scratch Team blog, "We're changing our name"](https://medium.com/scratchteam-blog/we-re-changing-our-name-5150e73c6dd9)
- Early funding came from NSF (a 2003 award to Resnick, Kafai and Maeda) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))
- Google.org has given general operating support since 2016, a $500,000 grant in June 2023 to widen access for underserved groups, and $5 million for a Chicago Public Schools-linked effort (as reported) — [K-12 Dive press release, 2023-06-05](https://www.k12dive.com/press-release/20230605-the-scratch-foundation-receives-500000-grant-from-google-to-expand-access/); [Scratch Foundation, Google grant 2023](https://www.scratchfoundation.org/scratch-google-grant-2023)
- Supporters listed at $1,000,000+ for 2021–2022 include Google.org, the LEGO Foundation and Siegel Family Endowment — [Scratch Foundation supporters](https://www.scratchfoundation.org/get-involved/supporters)
- In 2025 Scratch Memberships were added as a donation tier. On 7 April 2026 the Foundation announced a "Next Generation Platform", a bridge from 3.0 toward 4.0, planned for the end of 2026 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))
- ScratchJr came out for iPad in July 2014 and for Android in 2016. It targets ages 5–7 and is a full rewrite, not a port — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))
- ScratchEd is an educator community developed and supported by the Harvard Graduate School of Education. There are also Scratch Educator Meetups. Harvard's CS50 uses Scratch in its first week. It is used in 800+ DAV schools in India — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))
- Scratch Week (formerly Scratch Day) is held each May with events hosted by the community; the 2024 report counts 800+ events in 30 countries — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language)); [2024 Annual Report](https://annualreport.scratchfoundation.org/pdf/Scratch-Report_2024.pdf)
- Culture of play: every April Fools' Day the team adds pranks (for example turning event blocks into cat blocks) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_(programming_language))

### Inferences
- The non-profit, free, browser-based model removed the three usual barriers to school adoption: cost, installation and licensing reviews.
- A product for ages 5–7 (ScratchJr) and a course at Harvard (CS50) mean Scratch covers both ends of the age range, so it can become a shared reference point for teachers.

### Gaps
- Hour of Code / Code.org's specific role (Scratch tutorials in Hour of Code), the Scratch Conference (held at MIT in 2008, 2010, 2012, and Scratch@MIT events), Code Club / Raspberry Pi Foundation Scratch curricula: my searches did not return primary sources, so I make no claims about them here.
- How much translation is volunteer-driven: not sourced.

## Game-making as the dominant genre; mods and community extensions

### Takeaway
Games are a major category (one of six in the standard taxonomy), but I found no sourced overall percentage. Community-built tooling has formed its own ecosystem: TurboWarp (compiled to JavaScript, its own cloud server, an extension gallery, a packager to standalone apps) and forks such as PenguinMod. Cloud variables are the multiplayer route despite their limits.

### Cited Findings
- Graßl, Geldreich and Fraser (MSR 2017), as cited by a TU Delft study, sort Scratch projects into six categories: Game, Animation, Interactive Art, Music and Dance, Story, Video Sensing. They analyse differences by gender. The TU Delft work notes that project types are currently classified by hand through tags — [TU Delft repository, "What are the types of projects that Scratch users create?"](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:040a1a1f-ad4f-40a4-a714-1cfd1916a77a)
- A forum guess that about 10% of Scratch projects are platformers is anecdotal — [Discuss Scratch](https://scratch.mit.edu/discuss/topic/850734/?page=1)
- TurboWarp says it compiles projects to JavaScript and runs them "10–100x faster" (the developer's own claim). It is not affiliated with Scratch. Projects that use some of its extensions cannot be opened in Scratch or uploaded to the website — [TurboWarp Desktop](https://desktop.turbowarp.org/); [Flathub listing](https://flathub.org/ko/apps/org.turbowarp.TurboWarp); [TurboWarp Extension Gallery](https://extensions.turbowarp.org)
- TurboWarp runs its own cloud-variable server independent of Scratch. Users report that its cloud variables get deleted after a while, so lasting leaderboards are not possible — [TurboWarp docs, cloud variables](https://docs.turbowarp.org/cloud-variables); [Discuss Scratch](https://scratch.mit.edu/discuss/post/8341312/)
- The TurboWarp Packager turns projects into standalone HTML or desktop apps — [TurboWarp packager README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@turbowarp/packager@3.13.0/OLD_README.md)
- Snap! users built cloud variables modelled on TurboWarp's, which shows the idea spreading across block languages — [Snap! forum](https://forum.snap.berkeley.edu/t/cloud-variables-based-on-turbowarp/12319)

### Inferences
- The most popular community tools fix performance and distribution (export to a real app), the two things an ambitious game-maker outgrows first. That is the "high ceiling" problem.
- Mods that fork the format (TurboWarp extensions, PenguinMod) break uploads back to the main site. Format compatibility is the boundary between an ecosystem and fragmentation.

### Gaps
- The share of projects that are games, and the genre distribution: not found in a primary source (MSR 2017 full text not read).
- User counts for Scratch Addons, PenguinMod and TurboWarp: not found.
- Popular genres ("platformer engines", clicker games, RPGs, "Griffpatch tutorials"): not sourced in this pass.

## Lessons from comparable creative platforms (Roblox, Mario Maker, LittleBigPlanet, Dreams, Kodu, MakeCode, Game Lab)

### Takeaway
Platforms that combine making with a large *playing* audience and an economy (Roblox) or a simple, famous form (Mario Maker) scale. Platforms where discovery gets gamed (LBP's hearts trophy), or that depend on one console and one publisher (Dreams, LBP servers), lose their communities when support ends. Discovery design, a long-lived platform and portable content matter as much as tools.

### Cited Findings
- Roblox, 2025: 127 million average DAU (full year) across more than 180 countries, about 1.8 million daily unique paying users — [Roblox 10-K FY2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1315098/000131509826000024/rblx-20251231.htm). Q4 2025 averaged 144 million DAU (+69%) — [PYMNTS, 2026](https://www.pymnts.com/earnings/2026/roblox-posts-breakout-q4-after-chasing-older-core-gaming-market/). More than $1.5 billion in Developer Exchange fees in 2025 — [Roblox FY2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1315098/000110465926044380/rblx-20251231xars.pdf). More than 24,500 DevEx participants, with a median payout of $1,575 for the 12 months to 31 Dec 2024 — [Roblox FY2024 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1315098/000155837025004965/rblx-20250331xars.pdf) (as summarised by search excerpts)
- Super Mario Maker 2: more than 10 million courses uploaded by 7 January 2020, about six months after its June 2019 release. Nintendo raised the per-player upload cap (to 100). The game had sold 3.93 million copies by then — [Gematsu, 2020-01](https://www.gematsu.com/2020/01/super-mario-maker-2-tops-10-million-uploaded-courses); [Nintendo Wire, 2020-01-07](https://nintendowire.com/news/2020/01/07/super-mario-maker-2s-level-upload-cap-increased-to-celebrate-10-million-courses-being-uploaded/); [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/mario-maker-2-increases-course-limit-100/)
- LittleBigPlanet: Media Molecule's community manager James Spafford (GDC Taipei) described early sharing missteps. A trophy tied to reaching the maximum hearts on a level encouraged heart spamming, which made the ratings nearly useless for discovery. Sony was criticised for deleting player levels and then published its moderation guidelines — [Game Developer, "Media Molecule finds success and failure alike in LittleBigPlanet's sharing systems"](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/media-molecule-finds-success-and-failure-alike-in-i-little-big-planet-i-s-sharing-systems); [Game Developer, "Sony lays out LittleBigPlanet content policy"](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/sony-lays-out-i-littlebigplanet-i-content-policy). LBP3's servers were taken offline and later shut down permanently — [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/sony-confirms-littlebigplanet-3-shutdown-months-after-it-was-temporarily-taken-offline/). Softpedia reported that sharing drove LBP's success, with 6.7 million levels created (date in the article) — [Softpedia](https://news.softpedia.com/news/Sharing-Led-to-Little-Big-Planet-Success-6-7-Million-Levels-Created-278113.shtml)
- Dreams: Media Molecule ended live-service support in September 2023 (announced April 2023). Commentators cite a lack of support from Sony and reach limited to one console. The copyright policy removed content when rights holders asked. Media Molecule widened content usage rules before the service ended — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/media-molecule-expands-i-dreams-i-content-usage-rules-before-service-shutdown); [The Escapist](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/dreams-is-a-creative-triumph-and-legal-nightmare/); [SVG](https://www.svg.com/302897/the-reason-one-of-2020s-best-releases-is-struggling-to-stay-alive)

### Inferences
- Discovery is the hard problem in every UGC system. LBP shows that a gamified rating gets gamed. Scratch's mix of human curation (featured projects, curated studios) and organic signals is the counterexample.
- Locking content to one platform kills communities when the platform ends (LBP3, Dreams). Scratch's open file format (`.sb3`) and its CC BY-SA licence (until 2026) let mods like TurboWarp preserve and extend content.
- Roblox shows that a monetary economy scales a creator base enormously, but the median DevEx payout is small. That is a different motivation model from Scratch's intrinsic and social one, and it brings well-documented child-safety problems (not researched here).

### Gaps
- Kodu Game Lab, Minecraft Education / MakeCode Arcade and Code.org Game Lab: my searches returned nothing usable on their community dynamics, so I make no claims.
- LBP total level counts at shutdown, and Dreams sales or creator counts: not found in primary sources.

### Implications for our engine
(Synthesis for design, based on the findings above; not cited facts.)

1. **Make "share" and "see inside" a single format concern from day one.** Scratch's learning results depend on every shared project being readable and remixable. Our text formats (`sage-scene v1` plus plain Python) are already human-readable and diff-friendly, which is better than Scratch's JSON in a zip. Keep a shared game as a directory or archive of plain text plus assets, never a compiled blob.
2. **Put remix provenance in the project format.** Add a small, versioned `remix-of` record (parent id, root id, author, date) plus a **human-written credits/notes field**. "Computers can't give credit" says automatic links alone are not enough. Changing a file format needs Morgan's approval (CLAUDE.md), so this is a proposal.
3. **Choose the licence early and put it in the file.** Scratch's January 2026 move away from CC BY-SA created confusion for 135 million users' work. A per-project licence field with a permissive default makes remixing legal across tools and outlives any hosting site.
4. **Design discovery against gaming.** Avoid rewards tied to like counts (LBP). Reward transformative remixes (for example, show a remix's diff size relative to its parent) to address the remixing dilemma.
5. **Safety by construction for networked features.** If we add multiplayer or shared state, follow the cloud-variable pattern: typed, bounded, non-text channels by default, gated behind trust tiers. This also suits our deterministic, input-as-data model: synchronising input recordings is safer than free text.
6. **Plan for the ceiling (performance and export).** The biggest Scratch mods fix speed and standalone export. Our Rust core and native builds already address what TurboWarp was made for; make "export to a standalone game" an obvious action.
7. **Stay portable so the community can outlive the host.** Dreams and LBP lost their content with their servers. Open formats, local-first projects and an optional website keep the community's work alive.
8. **Institutional reach is non-technical.** Free, no install (a web build), translations from the start (string tables already exist in `sage_scene`) and educator material were the multipliers for Scratch; they cost little if the UI and the block palette are localisable from the beginning.
