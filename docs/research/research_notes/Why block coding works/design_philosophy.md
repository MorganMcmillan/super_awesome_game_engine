# Design philosophy and learning theory behind block-based environments

Scope: *why* Scratch-style environments work, according to their designers and the HCI/CS-education literature. Block mechanics (shapes, sockets, colors, palettes, round-tripping) are covered in the design doc and are deliberately not re-described here, except where a mechanic is the evidence for a principle.

Source note: the MIT Media Lab host (web.media.mit.edu) refused connections during this research; the Maloney 2010, CACM 2009 and IDC 2005 papers were read in full from Internet Archive copies of those exact URLs. The canonical URLs are cited below. Papert's own "Situating Constructionism" text could not be retrieved (see Gaps).

## 1. Constructionism, "low floor / high ceiling / wide walls", and the Four P's, and how they became Scratch design decisions

### Takeaway
Scratch is an explicit descendant of Papert's constructionism (learn by making things that matter to you, with others). Resnick's group re-weighted Papert's "low floor, high ceiling" toward **low floor and wide walls**, explicitly *de-prioritizing* the ceiling, and organized Scratch around three principles (more tinkerable, more meaningful, more social), later restated as the Four P's (Projects, Peers, Passion, Play). Each maps onto concrete product decisions: 2D instead of 3D, media import and paint tools, a single kit spanning many genres, a website launched with the language, and Creative Commons remixing.

### Cited Findings
- Scratch "builds on the constructionist ideas of Logo [Kafai and Resnick 1996; Papert 1980] and Etoys"; its abstract frames the goal as learning programming "while working on personally meaningful projects such as animated stories and games", with "self-directed learning through tinkering and collaboration with peers" as a key design goal. — [Maloney et al. 2010, TOCE](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf) (DOI [10.1145/1868358.1868363](https://doi.org/10.1145/1868358.1868363))
- The CACM paper attributes "low floor" (easy to get started) and "high ceiling" (increasingly complex projects over time) to Papert, adds "wide walls" (supporting many different types of projects so people with many different interests and learning styles can all become engaged), and says "Satisfying the triplet ... hasn't been easy." — [Resnick et al. 2009, "Scratch: Programming for All", CACM 52(11)](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf) (DOI [10.1145/1592761.1592779](https://doi.org/10.1145/1592761.1592779))
- The team's stated aim: "make the floor even lower and the walls even wider while still supporting development of computational thinking", via three core design principles: "Make it more tinkerable, more meaningful, and more social than other programming environments." — [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)
- Explicit statement of the ceiling tradeoff: "We plan to keep our primary focus on lowering the floor and widening the walls, not raising the ceiling." For learners heading to CS careers "it is important to move on to other languages", but for those who "see programming as a medium for expression, not a path toward a career, Scratch is sufficient ... A little bit of programming goes a long way." — [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)
- Diagnosis of why 1980s Logo/BASIC initiatives faded: languages "too difficult to use" (syntax); programming introduced via activities "not connected to young people's interests" (e.g., prime-number lists); and contexts where "no one could provide guidance when things went wrong—or encourage deeper explorations when things went right." These three map one-to-one onto tinkerable / meaningful / social. — [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)
- "More meaningful" was operationalized as two design criteria, **diversity** (many project genres) and **personalization** (importing photos and music, recording voices, creating graphics). Concrete decision: "we decided to focus on 2D images, rather than 3D, since it is much easier for people to create, import, and personalize 2D artwork." — [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)
- Resnick & Silverman's ten construction-kit principles (pre-Scratch launch): Design for Designers; Low Floor and Wide Walls; Make Powerful Ideas Salient – Not Forced; Support Many Paths, Many Styles; Make it as Simple as Possible – and Maybe Even Simpler; Choose Black Boxes Carefully; A Little Bit of Programming Goes a Long Way; Give People What They Want – Not What They Ask For; Invent Things That You Would Want to Use Yourself; Iterate, Iterate – then Iterate Again. — [Resnick & Silverman, "Some Reflections on Designing Construction Kits for Kids", IDC 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)
- On walls vs ceiling: "we have put less emphasis on high ceilings and more emphasis on what might be called 'wide walls'." Success metric: "we consider diversity of outcomes as an indicator of success. If the creations from a class of students are all similar to one another, we feel that something has gone wrong." Kits define "a space to explore, not a collection of specific activities." — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)
- Design challenge stated precisely: features "specific enough so that kids can quickly understand how to use them (low floor), but general enough so that kids can continue to find new ways to use them (wide walls)." — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)
- "Design for designers": a counter-example is single-model LEGO kits (Star Wars ship) where "many kids build the model suggested on the package ... and nothing more"; that is "hands-on learning" but "not ... what we mean by 'learning-through-designing.'" — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)
- Papert's "powerful ideas" = ideas that "can be used as tools to think with over a lifetime"; Papert complained educators read *Mindstorms* "as if the third term [powerful ideas] was there as a sound bite." The kit principle: let learners *encounter* powerful ideas (feedback, emergence) as a natural part of design rather than teaching them directly ("microworlds", e.g., StarLogo traffic jams and flocks). — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)
- The Four P's: **Projects** ("People learn best when they are actively working on meaningful projects – generating new ideas, designing prototypes, refining iteratively"), **Peers** ("Learning flourishes as a social activity"), **Passion** ("When people work on projects they care about, they work longer and harder, persist in the face of challenges"), **Play** ("playful experimentation – trying new things, tinkering with materials, testing boundaries, taking risks, iterating again and again"). Described as "strongly aligned with (and inspired by) the Constructionist approach ... learners playfully creating personally-meaningful projects in collaboration with peers." Developed with Natalie Rusk and Philipp Schmidt for the Learning Creative Learning course. — [Resnick, "Give P's a Chance", Constructionism 2014](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/constructionism-2014.pdf)
- Projects vs puzzles: Resnick criticizes puzzle-style Hour of Code tutorials: "learning to code by solving logic puzzles is like learning to write by solving crossword puzzles." The Scratch Hour of Code tutorial was project-based (interactive holiday cards). — [Resnick 2014](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/constructionism-2014.pdf)
- The "creative learning spiral": imagine, create, play, share, reflect, imagine again. — [Resnick 2014](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/constructionism-2014.pdf)
- Peers became product decisions: the online community launched *together* with the language in 2007; it serves as audience (feedback) and as inspiration (borrowing scripts and images); everything is Creative Commons; "More than a quarter of the projects on the Scratch website are remixes" (2014; it was "Over 15%" in 2009, and "Roughly one-third of the 2.5 million projects" in the tinkerability chapter). — [Resnick 2014](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/constructionism-2014.pdf); [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf); [Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- Minsky's line that "Logo had a great grammar but not much literature"; the Scratch site is "the beginning of a 'literature' for Scratch." — [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)
- Remixing initially upset some users ("stealing"); the site added automatic attribution links to originals, "derivatives" lists and a featured "Top Remixed" section to shift that norm. — [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)
- Play is redefined as an attitude: "taking risks, trying new things, and testing boundaries ... a process of tinkering, experimenting, and exploring." Supporting features: snap-apart blocks, the "backpack" for moving scripts/images/sounds between projects, and marking a project as a "draft". — [Resnick 2014](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/constructionism-2014.pdf)
- The name "Scratch" refers to hip-hop DJ scratching: tinkering with and mixing media. — [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Wide walls" is a stronger and more specific claim than "accessible": the kit is judged by the *variance* of what people make. For a game engine this argues against templates that funnel everyone into one genre and for primitives general enough to be bent into toys, art and music as well as games.
- The three historical failure modes (syntax, irrelevant tasks, no help) are the clearest design rubric in the literature; blocks solve only the first. The other two are content and community problems.
- The ceiling was a conscious sacrifice in Scratch, not an oversight (confirmed independently by Harvey & Mönig, section 6).

### Gaps
- Papert's own definition of constructionism ("Situating Constructionism", 1991, papert.org) could not be retrieved, so the definitions above are as restated by Resnick's group. The secondary Resnick restatement is consistent with the commonly quoted Papert text, but I did not verify the exact wording.
- *Lifelong Kindergarten* (MIT Press, 2017), the book-length Four P's treatment, was not consulted directly; the 2014 paper was used instead.

## 2. Tinkerability: bottom-up, experimental programming

### Takeaway
Tinkerability is a deliberate stance that bottom-up "conversation with the material" is a legitimate way of thinking, equal to top-down planning. The designers give it three principles (immediate feedback, fluid experimentation, open exploration), each realized by specific features: click any block, even in the palette; edit while running; no compile step or edit/run mode; glow on running scripts; live variable monitors; meaningful default arguments; a starter sprite with media; loose fragments allowed in the workspace.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: tinkering is "a valid and valuable style of working, characterized by a playful, experimental, iterative style of engagement"; tinkerers have "a conversation with the material" (citing Schön), often start bottom-up without a fixed goal, and "True tinkerers know how to turn their initial explorations (bottom) into a focused activity (up)." — [Resnick & Rosenbaum, "Designing for Tinkerability", in *Design, Make, Play* (2013)](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- Equity argument: schools privilege planners; citing Turkle & Papert, logic and planning should be "on tap" not "on top", and "Bricolage is a way to organize work. It is not a stage in a progression to a superior form." Goal: "end the privileged status of planning, and give equal emphasis to tinkering." — [Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- Responses to critics: tinkering lets learners build "fragments of knowledge that they can later integrate" (citing Hancock 2003); and the "too unstructured" critique "confuses tinkering with random exploration." — [Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- Related styles: Turkle's "hard" vs "soft" (also "patterners" vs "dramatists"); the LEGO/Logo amusement-park anecdote in which one group built a refreshment stand, wall, parking lot and people *before* finishing their Ferris wheel; the designers "pay special attention" to making their kits appealing to softs/dramatists because math and science "have traditionally been biased in favor of the hards." — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)
- **Three principles**: "immediate feedback, fluid experimentation, and open exploration." — [Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- Immediate feedback, *see the result*: "you can simply click on a programming block and watch what happens. There are no separate steps for compiling, no separate modes for editing ... You can even click on a block while it is still in the palette." Scripts can be changed while running ("changing the number in a move block ... or even insert a new block"); "This 'liveness' allows you to try out many possibilities ... in the context of a running program. The goal is to make people feel they can interact with programming blocks the same way they interact with physical objects." — [Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- Immediate feedback, *see the process*: scripts highlight while running; single-stepping highlights each block; variable and list monitors are "visible and manipulable" and users can type into list monitors while the program runs. — [Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- Fluid experimentation, *easy to get started*: launch with a default character (the cat) that already has two costumes and a sound; "The blocks start with reasonable default values for their inputs, so you can start playing with the blocks right away, without filling in any inputs." Setup code is identified as a barrier to tinkering. — [Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- Fluid experimentation, *easy to connect*: the LEGO "clutch power" analogy. Connectors should be easy to join and separate but hold firmly; wooden blocks are too loose and Erector sets too stiff. "If blocks snap together, they are sure to run." And: "you can leave some blocks (or stacks of blocks) lying around your workspace ... unlike most programming environments, where you need to keep your workspace tidy." — [Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- Open exploration: a variety of *materials* (media library, paint editor, camera, recorder, drag-in import, remixable community projects) and of *genres* (stories, games, animations, simulations, art, music). Specific block families tune for each genre (say/think for stories, collision/keys for games, pen for art, math for simulation, note/tempo for music), and because they share one kit "a single Scratch project can blend together multiple genres", e.g. "transforming an animation into a game." Contrasted with GameMaker and Gamestar Mechanic, which "focus explicitly on games." — [Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- Context matters as much as kit: "designing contexts for tinkerability is as important as designing kits." Lessons: process over product; themes, not challenges; diverse examples; arrange the space; engage people as well as materials; pose questions instead of giving answers; combine diving in with stepping back. — [Resnick & Rosenbaum 2013](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/designing-for-tinkerability.pdf)
- From the language paper: "A key feature of Scratch is that it is always live [Maloney and Smith 1995]. There is no compilation step or edit/run mode distinction ... By eliminating potentially jarring mode switches and compilation pauses, Scratch helps users stay engaged." Tinkerability "supports a bottom-up approach to writing scripts where small chunks of code are assembled and tested, then combined into larger units." Reporter blocks show their value "in a cartoon-like 'talk bubble'" when clicked; "each block comes with default parameters that give an illuminating demonstration of what that block does." Fragments left in the scripting pane are saved and act "similar to commented-out code"; long scripts can be broken apart and tested piecewise. — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- The liveness lineage is Maloney & Smith's Morphic work (UIST '95, "Directness and liveness in the morphic user interface construction environment"); Snap! still uses Morphic, which Maloney developed. — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf); [Snap! Reference Manual](https://docs.snap.berkeley.edu/snap-manual.pdf)
- Bau et al. note that liveness gives blocks "a key benefit of interpreted text-based languages without a read-eval-print loop console separate from the editor. The program gradually grows as it is augmented by dropping in these fragments when they behave as desired." — [Bau et al. 2017, "Learnable Programming: Blocks and Beyond", CACM](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)
- Iteration as a design-process value, too: "we put a high priority on 'tinkerability' – we want to encourage kids to mess with the materials, to try out multiple alternatives, to shift directions in the middle of the process." The designers field-tested Scratch prototypes continually before launch. — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf); [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Click a block to run it" and "edit while running" are not conveniences. They remove the boundary between *authoring* and *observing*, which the designers see as what makes computation feel like a physical material.
- Allowing loose, unattached fragments is a deliberate tinkering affordance (a "messy desk"), not a lack of validation.
- Tinkerability is a property of the whole loop (latency, defaults, setup cost, connector "clutch", visibility), not of the block notation alone. A text editor with hot reload and visible state can capture much of it, and a block editor with a compile step loses much of it.

### Gaps
- No measured latency threshold for "immediate" was found in these sources.
- The sources describe liveness in a single-threaded, non-deterministic interpreter; none discusses liveness under deterministic, replayable simulation (our constraint).

## 3. What the founding papers say: explicit principles and tradeoffs

### Takeaway
Maloney et al. 2010 is the most explicit statement of Scratch's design decisions. The rationale is consistently "remove the reasons a beginner would stop": a single window, an always-visible palette, liveness, visible execution, **no error messages** (fail-soft primitives), concrete data, a minimal command set, sprite-local scripts, loosely coupled broadcasts, and a concurrency model that prevents most race conditions. Each choice is presented with its cost.

### Cited Findings
- Goal and design drivers: "A key goal of Scratch is to introduce programming to those with no previous programming experience. This goal drove many aspects of the Scratch design." Obvious choices: "a visual blocks language, the single-window user interface layout, and the minimal command set"; less obvious: "how the target audience influenced the type system and the approach to error handling." The environment was designed "to invite scripting, provide immediate feedback for script execution, and make execution and data visible." — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- **Single window**: "a single-window, multi-pane design to ensure that key components are always visible. Scratch avoids floating palettes, which can get buried, and minimizes the use of panes that show only on demand." "To invite scripting, the command palette is always visible." Commands are divided into eight categories so that "in most palettes, all the commands can be viewed without scrolling", with "the most self-explanatory and useful commands" at the top. — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- Related anecdote: early users asked for movable, resizable panes; the team built it, "but users weren't happy with that either. What we needed to do ... was to fine-tune the parameters (i.e., adjust the sizes of the panes), not provide full flexibility. Often, designs with well-chosen parameters are more successful than designs with fully-adjustable parameters." — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)
- **Making execution visible**: running scripts get "a glowing white border"; on a runtime error (e.g., divide by zero) "the border turns red and the block that caused the error is highlighted in red"; single-stepping makes blocks flash; "Even when single-stepping mode is not enabled, Scratch updates the display after every command." — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- **No error messages / fail-soft**: "When people play with LEGO bricks, they do not encounter error messages." "Scratch has no error messages. Syntax errors are eliminated because ... blocks fit together only in ways that make sense. But Scratch also strives to eliminate run time errors by making all blocks be failsoft. Rather than failing with an error message, every block attempts to do something sensible even when presented with out-of-range inputs" (e.g., "set size" clamps its range). Rationale: "even when a script does not do the right thing, it does something, and that is a good start. A program that runs, even if it is not correct, feels closer to working than a program that does not run." The authors concede: "eliminating error messages does not eliminate errors." — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- Note the nuance: "no error *messages*" coexists with a red *visual* error indication on the offending block. — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- **Making data concrete** (after Boxer, diSessa & Abelson 1986): variable monitors on the stage, which double as score readouts and, with a slider, as controls; list monitors animate accesses. "Small design details can make a big difference": originally a new variable was not shown on the stage and "Many users did not discover variables"; after showing new variables by default, "many more users started using variables." — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- **Minimizing the command set**: "unlike a text-based language, every command consumes screen space in the command palettes, so there is a higher 'cost' to increasing the command set." Scratch 1.0 had 92 blocks and 1.4 had 125, "a constant struggle." Strategies: merge related operations into one block with a drop-down (math functions, image effects); make blocks "appear on demand" (WeDo motor blocks appear when the hub is plugged in, variable blocks after a variable exists); obsolete blocks stay supported at runtime so old projects run. — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- **Choosing black boxes**: the choice of primitives "determines, to a large extent, what ideas users can explore with the kit – and what ideas remain hidden." The turtle's `forward` hides trigonometry so users can explore geometry. Cricket's three-input RGB `setcolor` was replaced by a single 0–100 input so kids could write `forever [setcolor temperature]`: "the best way to deal with three dimensions was to throw away two of them." — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)
- "Make it as simple as possible – and maybe even simpler": the scaled-down Cricket (2 motors and 2 sensors instead of 4 and 6) won out; "What initially seems like a constraint or limitation can, in fact, foster new forms of creativity." — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)
- **Control structures as indivisible units**: "The closing arm of a loop or conditional block is part of the block itself—it cannot be misplaced." Instructors report students "think in Scratch blocks" as pseudocode after moving to text (Malan & Leitner 2007). — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- **Type system tuned to audience**: three first-class types (boolean, number, string); untyped variables; automatic number/string coercion ("Given the goals of Scratch, automatic conversion is preferable to requiring the user to explicitly convert"). Boolean slots are strict, number/string slots lenient. The paper anticipates that adding types could lead to "visual clutter"; one option is for new types to share the rounded shape, which "mirrors the choice between static and dynamic typing." — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- **Sprite-centric object model**: object-based, not object-oriented (no classes, no inheritance). "Commands operate only on the sprite in which they appear"; "An early prototype of Scratch allowed cross-sprite commands, but users found that confusing." Tradeoff: "The scripts in a given sprite tell the entire story about that sprite's behavior" and edits are local, but duplicating behavior (Breakout bricks) is tedious. Cloning was tried and deferred because of runaway clone floods, difficulty addressing only the new clone, and cleanup. — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- **Broadcasts instead of calls**: one-to-many, loosely coupled, asynchronous (plus a synchronous "and wait" variant). Benefits: debugging scope is limited to the sprite's own scripts and, "more importantly", sprites "can be moved between projects without breaking dependencies. This allows sprites to be shared." — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- **Concurrency without locks**: "our everyday world is highly concurrent, so Scratch users are not surprised that a sprite can do several things at once." Thread switches occur only "(1) on a command that waits explicitly ... or (2) at the end of a loop", never between an `if` test and its body, so users can "reason about a script in isolation." The residual problem is the arbitrary order of scripts triggered by the same event, solved by having one script trigger the others in order. — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- Harvey highlights the same thing as a strength: "from the beginning ... Scratch has had parallel processing ... This is a significant Big Idea that isn't so readily usable in 'grownup' languages." — [Harvey, "Why Do We Have to Learn This Baby Language?"](https://ftp.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/byob/site/doc/Brian-Harvey_Baby-language.pdf)
- Learnability claim: users "can be programming within fifteen minutes—yet with enough depth and variety to keep users engaged for years." The type system and object model "were designed to work smoothly without prior explanation, yet to make perfect sense on closer examination." — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- Comparison: Alice and Greenfoot target older users with class-based OOP and Java (AP preparation); Scratch "targets younger users ..., focuses on self-directed learning, and emphasizes tinkerability", includes its own paint and sound tools, and is "designed to encourage a wider variety of project types." — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- Design method: "Give People What They Want – Not What They Ask For". Observe rather than survey; users "ask for more flexibility than is really needed or desirable." Repeated "mistakes" are signals: LogoBlocks users kept dragging blocks back to the palette to delete them, so the designers made that gesture delete. — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)

### Inferences
- Fail-soft is a *per-primitive* design property (clamp, coerce, no-op), not an exception handler. It has to be designed into each primitive's semantics.
- The palette-cost argument means a palette generated from reflection is in direct tension with Scratch's most-cited discipline. Scratch's on-demand and drop-down strategies are the documented mitigations.
- Sprite-locality and broadcasts were chosen for *comprehensibility* ("the scripts tell the entire story") and *portability* (sharing). Any architecture that spreads one object's behavior across many places has to replace both.

### Gaps
- Maloney et al. give no quantitative user-study data for these decisions beyond the variable-visibility anecdote and adoption figures.
- The Scratch 3.0 (2019) design rationale was not covered; no primary paper on 3.0's design choices was found in this pass.

## 4. Recognition over recall, cognitive load, chunking, and natural-language block text

### Takeaway
The HCI case for blocks rests on three mechanisms (Bau et al. 2017): **recognition over recall** (persistent, topic-organized palettes; defaults and drop-downs for operands), **lower cognitive load through chunking** (one block = one meaningful unit), and **error prevention by constrained direct manipulation**. Students themselves most often credit the **natural-language** text of blocks, browsability and drag-and-drop. Documented costs are viscosity, low density, navigation, version control, and a perception of inauthenticity.

### Cited Findings
- Three learnability mechanisms: "1) favoring recognition over recall, 2) reducing cognitive load, and 3) preventing errors." "Picking a block from a palette is far easier than remembering a word"; "Blocks reduce the cognitive load by chunking code into a smaller number of meaningful elements"; blocks provide "constrained direct manipulation of structure (e.g., two incompatible concepts do not have connecting parts)." — [Bau et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)
- Vocabulary scale: simple languages have about 100–200 words ("HTML has 100 tags and 100 attributes, and SQL has about 200 keywords; Scratch is similar, with 130 blocks"); "Recalling 100-200 concepts can overwhelm a newcomer." — [Bau et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)
- Palettes vs autocomplete: palettes "persist instead of disappearing and they organize concepts by topic instead of by name. This design simplifies discovery and exploration." Operand burden ("order, type, and valid values") is handled by default values and drop-downs. — [Bau et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)
- Chunking: the JavaScript `for (var i = 0; i < 50; i++) {}` is "a total of seventeen units of information" against a working memory of "about seven chunks" (Miller); experts read it as two chunks; "Blocks help reduce cognitive load by showing new programmers how to read larger chunks" (Code.org renders the loop as one block plus its limit). — [Bau et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)
- Structure editing lineage: the 1981 Cornell Program Synthesizer authors (Teitelbaum & Reps): "Programs are not text; they are hierarchical compositions of computational structures and should be edited, executed, and debugged in an environment that consistently acknowledges and reinforces this viewpoint." — [Bau et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)
- Natural language: the graphical form "makes it possible to use natural language to describe the behavior of the command", e.g., Java `x=x+1;` vs a block reading "change x by 1." Students cited "the shape and visual layout of blocks, the ability to browse available commands, and the ease of the drag-and-drop composition", and the language: "Java is not in English it's in Java language, and the blocks are in English, it's easier to understand." — [Weintrop, "Block-based Programming in Computer Science Education", CACM 62(8), 2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop_CACM_2019.pdf)
- Harvey's version of the syntax argument: semicolons are required in Java, C and C++ but forbidden in Perl, Pascal and PL/I; "The visual layout of the code is the notation." — [Harvey, "Baby Language"](https://ftp.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/byob/site/doc/Brian-Harvey_Baby-language.pdf)
- Drawbacks: "visual programming languages can have a higher viscosity than text code because they make small changes harder" (e.g., `(a/2+b/2)` to `(a+b)/2` needs the tree pulled apart); "Low density"; "Search and navigation" in a 2D workspace; "Source control: collaboration and version control systems are difficult to use without a text representation." — [Bau et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)
- Mitigations: *text-style entry* (Greenfoot Stride, GP: inline autocomplete to insert blocks by typing; Stride uses text for expressions and blocks for structure) and *bidirectional mode switching* (Pencil Code, App Lab, BlockEditor, Tiled Grace). In dual-mode editors "the text code is the primary representation of the program, and blocks are a projected user interface view derived by parsing", which means the editor "must allow syntax errors that would not have been possible in blocks"; error-recovery heuristics turn simple errors into error blocks, "but complex errors can prevent mode switching." — [Bau et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)
- Perceived authenticity and power: "if we actually want to program something, we wouldn't have blocks"; "blocks are limiting ... There is not a block for everything." Weintrop: "the fact that students perceive this difference is a challenge educators face." — [Weintrop 2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop_CACM_2019.pdf) (study: [Weintrop & Wilensky, IDC 2015](https://researchr.org/publication/WeintropW15))
- Transfer: block-based students scored significantly higher after the introductory portion, but "there was no significant difference in scores" later in Java: gains "did not translate to students being further ahead when learning Java but also did not hamper their transition." Suggested future direction: "hybrid and bidirectional programming environments ... giving the learners agency for deciding how and when to switch." — [Weintrop 2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop_CACM_2019.pdf)
- Learnability beyond blocks: "blocks alone are not enough to make a programming language learnable." Install, save and load, runtime understanding (highlighting the running block, Snap!'s variable widgets) and liveness also matter. — [Bau et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)

### Inferences
- Recognition over recall depends on a palette that is browsable *and* bounded. Thousands of reflected entries turn the palette back into a recall problem (search), which is why curation matters (section 3).
- Natural-language block text is a separate lever from shapes. A block's label wording ("change x by 1") is a design artifact to write carefully, not something to derive mechanically from a Python identifier.
- Our lossless text-is-truth design is exactly Bau's "dual-mode" category, so it inherits their noted failure: complex text syntax errors block the projection back to blocks.

### Gaps
- Primary cognitive-load-theory studies specific to blocks (e.g., controlled working-memory measurements) were not found in this pass; Bau et al. argue from Miller's capacity estimate rather than from a blocks-specific experiment.
- The full text of Weintrop & Wilensky 2015 was not retrieved; quotes come via Weintrop's 2019 CACM column.

## 5. Intrinsic motivation, self-expression, media-rich computation, personally meaningful projects

### Takeaway
The designers treat motivation as an *input* to learning, not a reward: people "work longer and harder" on things they care about, and learners pull in concepts (variables, for example) when a project needs them. Media is the main channel for personal meaning. Guzdial's Media Computation, a separate line of evidence from university CS1, found large retention gains from a media context, while being candid that learning-outcome hypotheses were not supported and the mechanism is unproven.

### Cited Findings
- "When people work on projects they care about, they work longer and harder, persist in the face of challenges, and learn more in the process." Project diversity is read as evidence of passion: "This diversity of projects is a reflection of the diversity of interests of young people." — [Resnick 2014](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/constructionism-2014.pdf)
- Need-driven concept acquisition: Raul, 13, built a game but could not keep score; shown a variable, he "immediately saw how he could use it for keeping score ... then reached out and shook the researcher's hand, saying 'Thank you, thank you, thank you.'" Authors: "how many eighth-grade algebra teachers get thanked by their students for teaching them about variables?" Generalized: "As Scratchers work on personally meaningful projects ... they are ready and eager to learn important mathematical and computational concepts related to their projects." — [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)
- Media rationale: "we emphasized the use of media (music, sounds, graphics, photos) since we know that many young people are passionate about media." A skeptical parent's testimony: kids "could add THEIR own pictures and THEIR own voices." — [Resnick 2014](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/constructionism-2014.pdf)
- Scratch "added programmability to media-manipulation activities that are popular in youth culture", originally for after-school Computer Clubhouses, with "less focus on direct instruction." — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- Digital fluency framed as reading vs writing: young people "can 'read' but not 'write'"; fluency "requires not just the ability to chat, browse, and interact but also the ability to design, create, and invent with new media." — [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)
- Audience as motivation: "the opportunity to put their projects in front of a large audience—and receive feedback and advice from other Scratchers—is strong motivation." — [Resnick et al. 2009](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/Scratch-CACM-final.pdf)
- Respect for learners' own motivation: designers should "invent things that you would want to use yourself" and are "skeptical of educational software that ... requires that kids annotate each action." — [Resnick & Silverman 2005](https://web.media.mit.edu/~mres/papers/IDC-2005.pdf)
- Media Computation (Georgia Tech, from 2003, taught in Python): students "manipulate pixels to create Photoshop-like image effects, samples to splice or reverse sounds." Before it, non-engineering majors "failed or withdrew ... at a rate of over 50%"; the pilot had "less than 12%" withdraw or fail; over six years (4,292 students) the failure rate stayed "below 15%." — [Guzdial, "Exploring Hypotheses about Media Computation", ICER 2013](https://carpentries-i18n.github.io/instructor-training/files/papers/guzdial-mediacomp-retrospective-2013.pdf)
- Guzdial's own caveats: "good support for the retention hypothesis and for the gender hypothesis", but "Our results lead us to reject the learning hypothesis and the more-computing hypothesis." On mechanism: "I don't have a convincing explanation for the retention effect. My current hypothesis is that the context leads to a sense of relevance which leads to motivation ... But not all contexts are equally successful." Unexplored alternatives include "the creativity of MediaComp assignments." — [Guzdial 2013](https://carpentries-i18n.github.io/instructor-training/files/papers/guzdial-mediacomp-retrospective-2013.pdf)
- The NSF "Georgia Computes!" alliance built on this, using "motivating contexts (like media, story-telling, and robotics) to engage a broader audience." — [Guzdial 2013](https://carpentries-i18n.github.io/instructor-training/files/papers/guzdial-mediacomp-retrospective-2013.pdf)

### Inferences
- Games are already a high-passion medium, but the Scratch evidence says the lever is *personal* content (my drawings, my voice, my pet) more than genre. Fast asset import and in-editor creation tools are part of the motivational design.
- Guzdial's results support "context keeps people in the course", not "context makes them learn more per hour." Claims about our engine should be framed the same way.

### Gaps
- No primary self-determination-theory (Deci & Ryan) analysis specific to Scratch was retrieved; the motivation claims above are the designers' and Guzdial's.
- Guzdial's 2013 retrospective defines the "learning" and "more-computing" hypotheses in detail; I quote only their rejection and did not extract their exact definitions.

## 6. What was deliberately left out, and what Snap!'s designers argued about the ceiling

### Takeaway
Scratch shipped without procedures (removed after field tests confused them with broadcasts), without first-class lists or nested lists, without classes or inheritance, without directed messages or return values, and with cross-sprite commands removed, all deliberately, for comprehensibility by young children. Harvey and Mönig (BYOB, then Snap!) argued that a few additions grounded in Scheme's principle of **first-class everything** restore a near-unlimited ceiling *without* cluttering the floor, and that splitting the community into kid and adult languages would be a loss.

### Cited Findings
- **Procedures removed**: "Early versions of Scratch had a mechanism for creating procedures. In early field tests, however, many users were confused by procedures since they seemed very similar to broadcasts—both involved associating a name with a collection of commands. In the interest of simplicity and minimalism, procedures were removed from the language before Scratch was officially released." The authors acknowledge that "procedural abstraction is one of the 'powerful ideas' of computer science" and cite Harvey & Mönig's BYOB variant. — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- Custom blocks returned in Scratch 2.0 only in limited form: "A limited version of this capability, allowing only command blocks, later became part of Scratch 2.0." — [Harvey, "Baby Language"](https://ftp.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/byob/site/doc/Brian-Harvey_Baby-language.pdf)
- Harvey & Mönig: Scratch "lacks procedures, so it can't convey ... recursion" and "Its support for data structures is also weak. These weaknesses aren't oversights; the designers of Scratch deliberately avoided cluttering the language with anything a child might find threatening." Broadcasts can't be directed to one sprite, and handler scripts "can't take arguments or return values. These limitations are a deliberate part of the Scratch design, which has a primary goal that every aspect of the language should be intuitive even to young children." Lists arrived in 1.3, visible on stage by default; "Deep structures (lists of lists) are ruled out." — [Harvey & Mönig, "Bringing 'No Ceiling' to Scratch: Can One Language Serve Kids and Computer Scientists?", Constructionism 2010](https://www.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/BYOB.pdf)
- The one-language thesis: "it has been proposed to split the Scratch community with two versions of the language, one for kids and one for advanced users. We believe that this is not necessary. By taking key ideas, such as procedures as first class data, from the Scheme language, we can add only a few features to Scratch and still make it powerful enough to support a serious introductory computer science curriculum." Conclusion: the additions "will be inobtrusive", and "there are many benefits to keeping the most expert Scratch programmers as part of the same community as the beginners; a separate Scratch for older users would lose those benefits." — [Harvey & Mönig 2010](https://www.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/BYOB.pdf)
- Strachey's first-class criteria (variable value, aggregate member, argument, return value). With lists of lists "any other desired data structures can be implemented ... Thus, there is no need to clutter the basic Scratch menus with such derived types; they can be defined in libraries that can be loaded only when needed." Procedures-as-data (the ring, with RUN and CALL) let MAP, KEEP and new control structures be written *in the language* rather than added as primitives. OOP falls out: closures as instances, dispatch procedures for messages, delegation for inheritance. — [Harvey & Mönig 2010](https://www.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/BYOB.pdf)
- Blocks make abstraction *less* intimidating: "the graphical interface of Scratch makes the reification of procedures as data seem much less abstract and intimidating to novices"; a list of block images "eliminates the potentially confusing translation from the language's syntax ... to its semantics." — [Harvey & Mönig 2010](https://www.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/BYOB.pdf)
- Typed inputs that "help the user, not bind the user": custom-block inputs default to type Anything, and restrictive types are opt-in through a menu. — [Harvey & Mönig 2010](https://www.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/BYOB.pdf)
- Slogans: "Everything first class"; "Snap! is Scheme disguised as Scratch" (after "Logo is Lisp disguised as BASIC"). — [Harvey, "Baby Language"](https://ftp.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/byob/site/doc/Brian-Harvey_Baby-language.pdf); [Snap! Reference Manual](https://docs.snap.berkeley.edu/snap-manual.pdf)
- Custom blocks as ordinary blocks: Scratch keeps custom blocks in a separate "My Blocks" category with definitions visible in the scripting area, which treats them as "unusual" and is "practical only if you define a small number of custom blocks. If you have 40 custom blocks in a project, your scripting area will be unmanageable." Snap! files them in any palette category, hides definitions, and aims that "once you've written a block, you can forget it's homemade", to "emphasize the idea of abstraction, perhaps the central idea in computer science." Block libraries are written in Snap! itself. — [Harvey, "Baby Language"](https://ftp.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/byob/site/doc/Brian-Harvey_Baby-language.pdf)
- Prototype OOP over classes, *because of tinkering*: "classes aren't necessary, and are actually harmful in a dynamic, interpreted language that encourages tinkering. Class/instance programmers typically design their entire class hierarchy before actually writing code ... not for one kid designing a project on the fly." Clones share attributes with the parent until changed locally; inherited attributes are "ghosted" in the palette and watchers to reduce confusion. "Data hiding is less important than simulation." — [Harvey, "Baby Language"](https://ftp.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/byob/site/doc/Brian-Harvey_Baby-language.pdf)
- Performance hidden from the user: Snap! picks linked list vs dynamic array automatically according to which blocks are used ("users not have to know anything about data structures, but should still have their project run as fast as possible"). — [Harvey, "Baby Language"](https://ftp.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/byob/site/doc/Brian-Harvey_Baby-language.pdf)
- Respect for Scratch's choices: "In every case, the Scratch Team was aware of the possibilities that they decided to omit," and a choice "may be good for the Scratch target audience, but not for the Snap! target audience." — [Harvey, "Baby Language"](https://ftp.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/byob/site/doc/Brian-Harvey_Baby-language.pdf)
- Snap! is positioned for "a serious introduction to computer science" (high school and college; used in Berkeley's "Beauty and Joy of Computing"). — [Snap! Reference Manual](https://docs.snap.berkeley.edu/snap-manual.pdf); [Harvey & Mönig 2010](https://www.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/BYOB.pdf)
- Ceiling as escape hatch: a JavaScript-function block lets advanced users write libraries in JavaScript while "remaining in, and contributing to, the Snap! community." — [Harvey, "Baby Language"](https://ftp.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/byob/site/doc/Brian-Harvey_Baby-language.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Scratch–Snap! disagreement is about *audience and community*, not about whether abstraction is good. Both sides agree each feature must not leak complexity onto beginners' first screen. Snap!'s answer is "general mechanisms plus libraries, few new primitives"; Scratch's answer is "omit."
- Snap!'s "help, not bind" typing (Anything by default, opt-in restriction) is a concrete middle ground for typed sockets.
- Harvey's argument against classes is a tinkerability argument and maps well onto prefab-and-instance inheritance with local overrides (Snap!'s clone sharing is close to Flecs `IsA` with overridable components).

### Gaps
- I found no primary source for current (2026) Scratch 3 custom-block capabilities; it is widely reported that Scratch custom blocks still cannot report values, but that was not verified against a primary source in this pass.
- No published response from the Scratch team to Harvey & Mönig's one-language argument was found.

## 7. Implications for our engine

### Takeaway
Our design already matches Snap!'s ceiling argument (the ceiling is all of Python, and blocks are a view). The open risks lie on Scratch's side of the ledger: liveness under determinism, fail-soft semantics, palette size from reflection, and above all **locality**. ECS systems spread one entity's behavior across many queries, where Scratch deliberately keeps "the entire story" in one sprite.

### Cited Findings
(Each implication rests on the findings above; key citations repeated.)
- Liveness and no mode switch are central ("always live", "no compilation step or edit/run mode distinction") — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- Every command costs palette space; on-demand and drop-down consolidation are the mitigations — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- Sprite-locality ("tell the entire story") and loosely coupled broadcasts enable comprehension and sharing — [Maloney et al. 2010](https://web.media.mit.edu/~jmaloney/papers/ScratchLangAndEnvironment.pdf)
- Dual-mode editors: text is primary and blocks are a parsed projection; complex syntax errors block switching — [Bau et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09413)
- Students doubt blocks are "real" programming — [Weintrop 2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop_CACM_2019.pdf)
- Custom blocks should be ordinary, and the community should not be split — [Harvey, "Baby Language"](https://ftp.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/byob/site/doc/Brian-Harvey_Baby-language.pdf); [Harvey & Mönig 2010](https://www.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bh/BYOB.pdf)

### Inferences
1. **Click-to-run under determinism.** Scratch lets you click any block, even in the palette, and see it act. We should keep that, but a click mutates simulation state outside a tick. Options: run it in a throwaway fork (we already have snapshot/restore for play-in-editor) and show the result as a ghost; or record it as an editor command in undo history; or, during play, enqueue it as recorded `TickInput`-like data so replays stay valid. Reporter clicks (show the value in a bubble) are pure reads and safe; offer those everywhere.
2. **Edit while running = live reload, without losing state.** Scratch changes a `move` number mid-run. Our live reload must preserve the world (component data) across a script edit. Data lives in components, not Python variables (already a rule in CLAUDE.md), which is what makes this feasible.
3. **Fail-soft is a per-primitive contract.** Python raises, and Scratch's primitives do not. For block-facing native vocabulary, define clamped and coerced behavior (Scratch's `set size` clamp) and document it per block. Where an exception still happens, follow Scratch: the game keeps running, the offending system's block stack turns red, and the message goes in a side panel rather than a modal. Fail-soft behavior must itself be deterministic.
4. **Make execution and data visible.** Glow on systems and observers that ran this tick; per-block single-step highlighting during replay (our replays make "step back" possible, which Scratch never had); "monitors" = pin any component field to the game view, with an optional slider as a control. Lesson from the variable anecdote: show new things by default (a new component field appears in the inspector or as a monitor immediately), because undiscovered features go unused.
5. **Curate the reflected palette.** Reflection can generate hundreds of blocks; Scratch fought to stay near 100–130. Use drop-down consolidation (one "set [field] of [component]" block rather than one per field), on-demand blocks (component blocks appear when the component exists in the project, like Scratch's variable blocks and WeDo blocks), most self-explanatory blocks first in each category, and categories sized to avoid scrolling. Keep typed-entry insertion (Stride/GP-style autocomplete) for experts, to counter viscosity.
6. **Write block labels as natural language.** Students credit English-like labels ("change x by 1"). Reflection should supply *structure*, but each block needs an authored label template; identifier-derived labels lose a documented learnability lever.
7. **Locality is the biggest philosophical tension.** Scratch removed cross-sprite commands because users found them confusing, and argues that a sprite's scripts should tell its whole story. In ECS, "why does this paddle move?" is answered by *every system whose query matches it*. Mitigation: an entity-centric view in the inspector listing the systems and observers that match the selected entity (live, with glow), so the Scratch question has a one-click answer. Consider an authoring convention that groups a prefab's systems with the prefab, giving Scratch's "share a sprite without breaking dependencies" property (events and observers already play the role of loosely coupled broadcasts).
8. **Concurrency model.** Scratch's yield-only-at-waits-and-loop-ends model is close to our run-to-completion systems per tick, so most race reasoning transfers. What Scratch has and per-tick systems lack is a sequential "wait 1 second" / "forever" script. Whether to offer coroutine-style blocks (lowered to timer or state components so they remain hashed and snapshotted) is a design question to raise with Morgan, not to settle here. Also note Scratch's one residual concurrency bug class: unexpected ordering of handlers fired by the same event. Our StableId ordering makes this deterministic but still possibly surprising; surface the order visually.
9. **Loose fragments in the workspace.** Scratch deliberately lets unattached stacks lie around (a "messy desk", saved with the project). In a lossless Python view, a loose fragment needs a text representation (for example, an unregistered function or a marked block). Decide this explicitly, because it is a documented tinkering affordance.
10. **Low floor via defaults and a starter.** Every block needs an "illuminating" default argument, so a click does something visible. New projects should open with a ready character that has media and a sound (the Scratch cat), not an empty world.
11. **Ceiling: follow Snap!, not Scratch.** Our ceiling is Python, so Harvey's one-community argument applies directly: custom blocks (user Python functions) should appear in palettes as ordinary blocks in any category, with definitions hidden by default; typed sockets should default to "help, not bind." Prototype-style prefab inheritance with local overrides suits tinkering better than up-front class hierarchies (Harvey).
12. **Authenticity is an advantage to show.** Weintrop's students doubted blocks were real code. Showing the Python text beside the blocks (a live, linked view) answers that directly and gives learners agency over when to switch, which Weintrop names as the promising direction. Bau's warning applies: a syntax error in the text side can block the projection to blocks, so degrade gracefully with "error blocks" or a read-only fallback region.
13. **Wide walls inside a game engine.** Resnick contrasts Scratch with game-only kits. Judge success by diversity of output. Include primitives for toys, music, drawing (pen-like), storytelling (speech balloons) and simulations, so that "a game" can morph into something else, as with Scratch's genre blending.
14. **Context and community are half the design.** Remixing (15% in 2009, more than a quarter by 2014), a backpack for moving scripts and assets between projects, "draft" status (process over product), and themes rather than challenges for tutorials. These need sharing-friendly units: prefabs plus their systems plus their assets.
15. **Design method.** Observe users rather than survey them, and fix repeated "mistakes" by making the attempted gesture work (the LogoBlocks drag-to-palette-to-delete precedent). Prefer well-chosen fixed parameters over full configurability (the Scratch pane-resizing anecdote), which is relevant to the editor UI built on our UI module.

### Gaps
- None of the sources address block editing over a deterministic, replay-hashed simulation; points 1, 3 and 8 are our extrapolations and need design work.
- No source evaluates ECS-style (query-based) behavior with novices; the locality concern in point 7 is inferred from Scratch's sprite-locality rationale, not from a study.
