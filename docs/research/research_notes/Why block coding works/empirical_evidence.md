# Empirical evidence on block-based programming: effectiveness, limitations, transfer to text

Scope note: these notes cover the evidence, not block mechanics. Strength labels used below:
**Strong** = randomized or well-controlled, large N, or a meta-analysis; **Moderate** = quasi-experimental with controls (same teacher, same curriculum) or large log/repository analyses; **Weak** = small classroom case studies, single sites, slides, theses or secondary summaries. Most of this field is Moderate or Weak. There are few randomized trials, the samples are small, and follow-up is short. Researched November 2026.

## 1. Blocks vs text: what controlled comparisons show

### Takeaway
In the best-known isomorphic comparison (the same environment and curriculum, with only the modality changed), blocks produced larger short-term conceptual gains (d ≈ 0.58 at 5 weeks) and more interest in further CS, but no difference in enjoyment or confidence. Meta-analyses find small-to-moderate positive effects whose significance depends on the study pool. The advantage is real but modest, it is front-loaded, and it is clearest on completion and speed.

### Cited Findings
- **Weintrop & Wilensky 2017 (TOCE 18(1), Art. 3). Moderate evidence: quasi-experimental, N = 60, two classes of 30 in one selective-enrollment Chicago high school, same teacher, 5 weeks, Pencil.cc in blocks-only or text-only (CoffeeScript) mode.** Pre-test scores were equivalent (blocks 54.3%, text 51.6%). Post-test: blocks 66.6% (SD 13.4) vs text 58.8% (SD 14.6), t(52) = 2.03, p = .041, d = 0.58. Within-condition gains: blocks d = .96, text d = .50 — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2017 PDF](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf)
- Same study: blocks students outperformed text students even on *text*-format questions (Pencil Code text items d = .41, not significant). The only significant per-modality difference was on Pencil Code blocks items (d = .73). Students "performed comparably across the three modalities" rather than best in the modality they had just used. — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf)
- Same study, by concept: the only significant difference was on *comprehension* questions (what a whole program does), t(52) = 3.21, p = .002, d = .86. Blocks students rated conditionals, loops, functions and variables as easier, and perceived ease correlated with actual performance. — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf)
- Same study, attitudes: no difference in **enjoyment** between conditions (post U = 408, p = .62). The authors say "the narrative of block-based programming being more fun than text-based programming does not hold for high-school-aged learners in formal classrooms". **Interest in future CS** diverged: blocks 8.59/10 vs text 7.18/10 after 5 weeks (p = .04). Blocks students rated programming as *harder* afterwards (p = .01) even though they scored higher. The authors suggest the students did not count what they did in blocks as "programming". — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf)
- **Price & Barnes 2015 (ICER). Weak to moderate: middle-school novices in a summer program (SPARCS), blocks vs an otherwise identical text interface, one short activity.** Per the authors' slides, "by almost any measure, the Block interface improved performance": an equal or larger share of the blocks group completed each goal, and they finished faster. The authors warn that the sample may have been too small after dropout. Weintrop & Wilensky cite it as finding blocks increase confidence. — [Price 2015 publication page](https://isnap.csc.ncsu.edu/home/public/publication/price-2015); [author slides](https://www.sambuz.com/doc/comparing-textual-and-block-interfaces-in-a-novice-ppt-presentation-764257); [Weintrop & Wilensky 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf)
- **Bau, Gray, Kelleher, Sheldon & Turbak 2017, "Learnable Programming: Blocks and Beyond" (CACM 60(6), 72–80). A review and position piece, not new data.** It argues blocks help novices through recognition over recall, lower cognitive load and error prevention, and that prior blocks experience can help later text learning. It grew out of the VL/HCC 2015 Blocks and Beyond workshop (51 submissions). — [arXiv 1705.09413](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.09413)
- **Xu, Ritzhaupt, Tian & Umapathy 2019 (Computer Science Education 29(2–3)). Meta-analysis of 13 publications comparing blocks with text for novices.** Cognitive effect g = 0.245, affective effect g = 0.195. Both were *not statistically significant* under random-effects models. — [ERIC EJ1218045](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1218045); [T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08993408.2019.1565233)
- A 2020 meta-analysis of children's block-based courses (Springer LNCS chapter) found a significantly larger effect for block-based programming than for traditional instruction on overall learning outcomes. Note that this compares blocks with *instruction in general*, not with text. — [Springer 2020](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-60703-6_33)
- A three-level meta-analysis (reported as 105 studies and 539 effect sizes; the search summary attributes it to the ResearchGate paper "Exploring the Effectiveness and Moderators of Block-Based Visual Programming on Student Learning") found a large effect on problem-solving, small effects on programming skill and computational thinking, and a trivial effect on motivation, compared with traditional instruction. I could not verify the full text. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343282592_Exploring_the_Effectiveness_and_Moderators_of_Block-Based_Visual_Programming_on_Student_Learning_A_Meta-Analysis)
- **2025 meta-analysis, "To block or not to block?" (Research in Science & Technological Education), covering studies from 2001 to 2024, Hedges' g.** Positive effect on academic achievement. Interventions of 5–6 weeks were the most effective, and the largest benefits were in science-related applications. The authors call for long-term research. The summary did not give a pooled g. — [T&F DOI 10.1080/02635143.2025.2593936](https://doi.org/10.1080/02635143.2025.2593936)
- A recent (c. 2026) ScienceDirect meta-analysis of educational programming tools: overall g = .473, 95% CI [.388, .559]. Block-based tools (especially Scratch) and physical/robotic tools (LEGO Mindstorms) had larger effects than other tool types. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958826004434)
- **Weak, frame-based editing, which sits between blocks and text: Price, Brown, Lipovac, Barnes & Kölling 2016 (ICER), a controlled study of middle-school students comparing Java in Greenfoot's text editor with Stride (frames).** Frustration and satisfaction were similar. The Stride group progressed faster, completed more objectives, and spent significantly less time on purely syntactic edits and with non-compilable code. — [Price 2016 ICER page](https://isnap.csc.ncsu.edu/home/public/publication/price-2016-icer); [Academia copy](https://www.academia.edu/28871992/Evaluation_of_a_Frame_based_Programming_Editor)
- Altadmri, Kölling & Brown (COMPSAC 2016, "The Cost of Syntax and How To Avoid It") use Blackbox logs of BlueJ Java to *predict* the time frame-based editing would save on syntax and indentation errors. These are modeled estimates, not measured outcomes. — [Kent KAR](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/56259)

### Inferences
- The best-designed head-to-head study gives d ≈ 0.6 at 5 weeks, from a single school with N = 60. Meta-analyses that pool weaker studies land near g ≈ 0.25–0.5, and blocks-vs-text specifically (Xu 2019) is not significant. The honest summary is "a small-to-moderate, short-term advantage for novices", not a large or durable effect.
- The clearest gains are in comprehension of whole programs, completion and speed. Enjoyment shows no reliable difference, which undercuts the "blocks are more fun" claim for teenagers.
- The 2025 finding that 5–6 week interventions do best fits a picture in which blocks help most at the start.

### Gaps
- I found no large randomized controlled trial comparing blocks with text. Most comparisons involve a single classroom or a single school.
- I could not get pooled effect sizes or study counts for the 2025 RSTE meta-analysis, and I did not check whether the 2025 and 2026 meta-analyses draw on overlapping studies.
- I could not get exact statistics from the full Price & Barnes 2015 paper; the slides are a secondary source.

## 2. Transfer from blocks to text

### Takeaway
Blocks neither help nor hurt eventual performance in text: the advantage from blocks fades after the switch to a professional language, and Java error rates were the same. Dual-modality (blocks and text views of the same program) is the most promising bridge, with one large CS1 study (N = 673) showing exam gains. These studies are quasi-experiments across cohorts, not randomized.

### Cited Findings
- **Weintrop & Wilensky 2019 (Computers & Education 142, 103646). Moderate: the same 60 students followed for 10 weeks of Java after the 5-week introduction (15 weeks total).** The 5-week blocks advantage (66.6% vs 58.8%, d = 0.58) disappeared: post-Java scores were 64.9% vs 64.7%, t(57) = 0.014, p = .99. Gains from mid to post differed significantly (d = 0.70) because blocks students plateaued while text students kept improving. "All of the students started and ended in the same place… but the two conditions took different paths." — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2019 PDF](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop&Wilensky_2019_C&E.pdf)
- Same study, Java practice: compile error rates were the same. Text-origin students had errors on 53.7% of javac calls vs 53.2% for blocks-origin students (p = .57). The paper reports no differences in programming practices and no significant attitude differences at 10 weeks. One explanation it offers is "little transfer between environments for students coming from the block-based condition". — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop&Wilensky_2019_C&E.pdf)
- The 2019 paper also reviews earlier work: students with middle-school Scratch experience showed little quantitative difference in a later high-school text course but outperformed peers on some topics (iteration) and reported higher self-efficacy. Students taught with a transfer-focused curriculum did better in later Java courses. — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop&Wilensky_2019_C&E.pdf)
- **Blanchard, Gardner-McCune & Anthony 2020 (SIGCSE). Moderate, the largest N found: CS1 at the University of Florida, a text-only control semester (n = 248) vs a dual-modality semester (n = 425) using a Droplet-based blocks/text plugin, used by 88.9% of the intervention group.** Intervention students scored higher on code-reading and definitional exam questions (first midterm η² = 0.41, second midterm η² = 0.01, final η² = 0.03, all p < .001). On code writing they scored higher in the first midterm (η² = 0.03) but not in the second (p = .826). On the **SCS1 concept inventory there was no difference** (51.6% vs 50.1%, p = .46). Limitations: a different semester, partly different exams and a single course. — [Blanchard et al. 2020 PDF](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/775/2020/01/blanchard-et-al-SIGCSE2020.pdf)
- Blanchard et al. also report earlier middle-school work showing that students who moved from blocks to text through a dual-modality environment held more positive views of text programming than students who went directly from blocks to text. — [Blanchard et al. 2020](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/775/2020/01/blanchard-et-al-SIGCSE2020.pdf)
- **Weintrop & Holbert 2017 (SIGCSE), "From Blocks to Text and Back". Moderate: log analysis of Pencil Code with free switching between modes.** Learners shifted gradually from blocks to text over the course, and returned to blocks when new constructs appeared, which suggests blocks act as on-demand scaffolding rather than a stage to leave behind. — [Weintrop & Holbert 2017 PDF](https://ccl.netlogo.org/2017/Blocks.pdf); [Holbert & Weintrop companion](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/HolbertWeintrop.pdf)
- **Kazemitabaar et al. 2022 (IDC), CodeStruct, a dual-mode structured Python editor for K-12.** In the final transition to fully text-based Python, learners performed equally well, and slightly better in 10 of 16 activities. This is a small study. — [Kazemitabaar et al. IDC 2022 PDF](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Kazemitabaar_et_al_IDC_2022.pdf)
- **BlockPy (Bart et al.)** is a browser-based Python environment with a dual blocks/text view for data science, built on Skulpt. I found no outcome study on blocks-to-text transition. — [BlockPy (Academia)](https://www.academia.edu/71528446/BlockPy_An_Open_Access_Data_Science_Environment_for_Introductory_Programmers); [CompSAC 2016](https://people.cs.vt.edu/~tilevich/papers/compsac2016.pdf)
- **Misattribution warning.** A frequently surfaced "transfer" meta-analysis (g = 0.49, 95% CI [0.37, 0.61]; near transfer g = 0.75, far g = 0.47) is about whether *learning to program transfers to other cognitive skills*, not about blocks-to-text transfer. From memory, I believe it is Scherer, Siddiq & Sánchez Viveros 2019 (Journal of Educational Psychology). The search result did not confirm the authors. Do not cite it as blocks-to-text evidence. — [ResearchGate listing seen via search](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330380587_Block-based_versus_text-based_programming_environments_on_novice_student_learning_outcomes_a_meta-analysis_study)

### Inferences
- In the one longitudinal comparison, blocks bought speed early but did not change where students ended up. That is no transfer penalty, and also no transfer bonus.
- The positive evidence comes from environments that show *the same program* in both representations (Pencil Code/Droplet, CodeStruct). This matches the "blocks as a view of Python" design closely.
- Free switching plus a natural drift toward text, with returns to blocks for unfamiliar constructs, suggests the editor should make switching cheap and per-construct rather than a one-way graduation.

### Gaps
- I found no randomized trial of dual-modality vs blocks-only vs text-only that measures text performance months later.
- I found no published transition outcome for BlockPy, or for Snap!-to-Python specifically.
- I did not extract switching rates or percentages from Weintrop & Holbert 2017.

## 3. Bad habits and misconceptions

### Takeaway
Classroom studies in Israel documented habits that Scratch encourages and that conflict with accepted practice: bottom-up "tinkering" assembly and extremely fine-grained decomposition. Students learned most concepts but struggled with repeated execution, variables and concurrency. Repository-scale studies confirm structural problems (long scripts, duplication, dead code) and show that these "smells" measurably hurt children's comprehension.

### Cited Findings
- **Meerbaum-Salant, Armoni & Ben-Ari 2011, "Habits of programming in Scratch" (ITiCSE '11, 168–172; DOI 10.1145/1999747.1999796). Weak to moderate: observations in middle-school classrooms.** Scratch "engenders certain habits of programming": (a) a totally bottom-up development process that starts with individual blocks, and (b) extremely fine-grained programming, in which programs are split into many tiny scripts. The authors argue that both conflict with accepted CS practice. — [CRIS IUCC](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/habits-of-programming-in-scratch/); [Weizmann Scratch page](https://stwww1.weizmann.ac.il/scratch/?p=158)
- **Meerbaum-Salant, Armoni & Ben-Ari 2013, "Learning computer science concepts with Scratch" (Computer Science Education 23(3), 239–264). Moderate: pre/post tests built on a combination of revised Bloom and SOLO taxonomies.** Students "could successfully learn important concepts of CS, although there were problems with some concepts such as repeated execution, variables, and concurrency". The authors attribute the differences between concepts partly to how well each concept maps onto a Scratch construct. — [CRIS IUCC](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/learning-computer-science-concepts-with-scratch/); [Academia](https://www.academia.edu/41755792/Learning_Computer_Science_Concepts_with_Scratch)
- **Hermans & Aivaloglou 2016, "Do code smells hamper novice programming?" (ICPC). A controlled experiment with children: the same Scratch game in a clean version and in long-method and duplicated-code versions.** Children "performed significantly better on the non-smelly program", and the smells affected some tasks more than others. — [Aivaloglou ICER slides (summarizing ICPC 2016)](https://cs.ou.nl/oursi/OUrsi026-Aivaloglou-How%20Kids%20Code%20and%20How%20We%20Know.pdf)
- A 2024 ResearchGate paper, "From Blocks to Text: Bridging Programming Misconceptions", addresses whether misconceptions carry over from blocks to text. I read only the title and listing. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379520105_From_Blocks_to_Text_Bridging_Programming_Misconceptions)

### Inferences
- In Scratch the habits are partly an artifact of the design: event hat blocks invite many small scripts that run concurrently, and the free canvas invites assembly by tinkering. A Python view loses the "many tiny scripts" structure unless handlers are made just as cheap.
- Concurrency misconceptions in Scratch come from implicit parallel scripts. An ECS with explicit, ordered systems (sorted by StableId, fixed phases) avoids that source of confusion, but it needs to make the order visible.

### Gaps
- I could not access the full text or numbers of either Meerbaum-Salant paper, such as the per-concept posttest percentages.
- I found no study measuring whether these Scratch habits persist after the switch to text.

## 4. Large-scale analyses of Scratch repository projects

### Takeaway
Most public Scratch projects are small and simple. Procedures (custom blocks) and other abstractions are rare, and dead code and duplication are common. Remixing correlates with learning new concepts and a larger command vocabulary, but only about a tenth of projects are ever remixed.

### Cited Findings
- **Aivaloglou & Hermans 2016, "How Kids Code and How We Know: An Exploratory Study on the Scratch Repository" (ICER '16, 53–61). Moderate: 250,000 scraped projects, of which about 233,000 were non-empty.** Most projects are small, though some have more than 100 sprites. Procedures and other abstraction features are rarely used. Code smells are widespread. — [TU Delft record](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/how-kids-code-and-how-we-know-an-exploratory-study-on-the-scratch/); [SERG page](https://serg.ewi.tudelft.nl/publications/how-kids-code-and-how-we-know-an-exploratory-study-on-the-scratch-repository/)
- From the author's talk slides (labels partly unclear): 75% of non-empty projects have at most 5 sprites, 12 scripts and 76 blocks. Duplication appears across sprites in 26% of projects and within sprites in 10%. Scripts longer than 18 blocks appear in 30% of projects. A secondary citation reports dead code in more than 25% of projects. — [Aivaloglou slides](https://cs.ou.nl/oursi/OUrsi026-Aivaloglou-How%20Kids%20Code%20and%20How%20We%20Know.pdf)
- The dataset was released as "A dataset of Scratch programs: scraped, shaped and scored" (Aivaloglou, Hermans, Moreno-León & Robles, MSR 2017), 247K projects. A follow-up by Robles et al. 2017 studied copy-and-paste cloning in more than 230,000 projects. — [Aivaloglou slides](https://cs.ou.nl/oursi/OUrsi026-Aivaloglou-How%20Kids%20Code%20and%20How%20We%20Know.pdf)
- **Hill & Monroy-Hernández 2013, "The Remixing Dilemma" (American Behavioral Scientist). Moderate: quantitative analysis of Scratch.** Only about one tenth of projects are ever remixed. Moderate complexity, creator prominence and cumulativeness predict more remixing (generativity), but also *less original* remixes, measured by edit distance from the source. — [arXiv 1507.01295](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.01295); [MSR PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/hill_monroy-remixing_dilemma.pdf)
- **Dasgupta, Hale, Monroy-Hernández & Hill 2016, "Remixing as a Pathway to Computational Thinking" (CSCW '16, 1438–1449). Strong for this field as observational data: 2,426,894 projects by 1,068,502 users over Scratch's first five years.** Exposure to a CT concept through remixing is associated with a higher likelihood of using that concept. Users who remix more have larger command repertoires, controlling for the number of projects and the amount of code. The authors call the effects "modest". — [MSR page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=168717); [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/remixing-as-a-pathway-to-computational-thinking)
- **Dr. Scratch (Moreno-León, Robles & Román-González, 2015 onwards)** is automatic CT scoring on seven dimensions: abstraction, parallelism, logic, synchronization, flow control, user interactivity and data representation. In a CHI 2017 comparison of more than 450 evaluations by 16 experts, automatic and manual scores correlated strongly. — [RED 2015](https://revistas.um.es/red/article/download/240251/182971); [Moreno-León thesis, URJC](https://burjcdigital.urjc.es/handle/10115/15593)
- Critiques of Dr. Scratch: a measurement study of about 230,000 projects questions the scale's psychometric quality. Other work notes it cannot assess debugging, remixing, functionality or creativity. Counting features is not the same as assessing understanding. — [Bubnič, Univ. of Maribor Press](https://press.um.si/index.php/ump/catalog/view/886/1346/4484)

### Inferences
- Real users mostly build small, flat, event-driven programs. Abstraction (procedures) is the feature that is underused at the ceiling. An editor that wants a high ceiling has to make functions and reuse *easier* than copy-paste, or duplication will dominate.
- Remixing is associated with vocabulary growth, but most projects are never remixed, so discoverability matters.

### Gaps
- I could not get the exact per-concept usage percentages (loops, conditionals, variables, lists, broadcasts) from Aivaloglou & Hermans; the slide numbers lack labels.
- The repository studies mostly use 2010s data. I found no comparable recent analysis of Scratch 3 projects.

## 5. Perceptions of authenticity and the ceiling

### Takeaway
Some high-school learners see blocks as less authentic. The effect is concentrated among students with prior experience or career aims, and in the controlled study the averages did not differ significantly. Blocks users rated programming as harder, which suggests that what they did in blocks did not count as programming to them. I found little rigorous evidence on intermediate or advanced users.

### Cited Findings
- Weintrop & Wilensky 2017: "Pencil.cc is similar to what real programmers do", text 6.71 vs blocks 5.89 out of 10, not significant (p = .23). "Pencil.cc made me a better programmer": 7.79 vs 7.44 (p = .71). In free responses, one student said blocks were "a bit too limiting for someone who… [is] going to make something that is going to be used in industry". The authors conclude that "students with more prior experience or more serious intentions… appear to be more critical of the authenticity and utility of block-based introductory tools." — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf)
- The abstract of the same study: text students "viewed their programming experience as more similar to what professional programmers do and as more effective at improving their programming ability". This rests on the numeric differences above, which were not significant. — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf)
- Earlier work by the same authors (Weintrop & Wilensky 2015, "To block or not to block") found authenticity to be a drawback some high-school learners recognize, along with perceived limits on program size and power. — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2015 PDF](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2014/Weintrop%20and%20Wilensky%20-%202015%20-%20To%20Block%20or%20Not%20to%20Block%2C%20That%20is%20the%20Question%20St.pdf)
- Weintrop & Wilensky 2019 quote students who saw blocks as preparation for "real" languages. — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop&Wilensky_2019_C&E.pdf)

### Inferences
- A view that always shows real Python, and lets the user flip to it, directly addresses the authenticity objection: the blocks *are* the industry language.
- The ceiling problem in Scratch is partly a matter of perception, and partly real, given the rarity of procedures and the long scripts in repository data.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous quantitative study of perceived authenticity among adults, or of where intermediate users drop blocks.

## 6. Features that drive persistence and retention

### Takeaway
The quantitative evidence on retention in block communities is thin. The best evidence ties *giving feedback* and social bonding in a newcomer's first two weeks to later learning and recognition, and ties remixing to vocabulary growth. Blocks in a classroom raised stated intent to continue in CS (one study).

### Cited Findings
- **"Many Destinations, Many Pathways" (CSCW 2022; arXiv 2211.04046). Moderate: large observational study of Scratch newcomers' first 14 days.** Newcomers who posted feedback (comments on others' projects) had about 2.58 times the odds of later receiving "loves". Posting an original project in that window was associated with lower odds (0.49 times). Feedback exchange and social bonding were positively associated with learning about the community and its practice. The outcomes are learning and recognition proxies, not explicit retention. — [arXiv 2211.04046](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.04046)
- A TU Delft bachelor thesis (2024, Weak) found only a low correlation between the sentiment ratio of comments a creator receives and how long they stay active, and a low correlation between the number of comments received and the number of projects created. — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:86d2d81b-bb2e-4745-836b-df6a8428815d)
- Remixing correlates with concept uptake and command repertoire (Dasgupta et al. 2016, above). — [MSR page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=168717)
- Weintrop & Wilensky 2017: in class, blocks raised intent to take more CS (8.59 vs 7.18, p = .04). The paper links this to earlier work on retention with Alice (Moskal et al. 2004). — [Weintrop & Wilensky 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf)
- Hermans & Aivaloglou 2017 (WiPSCE): over 8 weeks, starting with Scratch vs starting unplugged made no difference in understanding of concepts, but the Scratch-first group had better self-efficacy and used a wider selection of blocks. — [Aivaloglou slides](https://cs.ou.nl/oursi/OUrsi026-Aivaloglou-How%20Kids%20Code%20and%20How%20We%20Know.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a game editor, social features such as sharing, commenting and remixing are associated with learning and recognition, but none of these studies shows that they *cause* retention.

### Gaps
- I found no causal (A/B) study of retention features in a blocks environment. Retention data from Scratch, MakeCode or Roblox is not public in a form I could find.

## 7. Blocks and visual scripting for adults and professionals

### Takeaway
Rigorous evidence is scarce. Unreal Blueprints is the main professional case, and the research is just beginning: datasets, defect prediction, and complexity metrics proposed in conference talks. I found no controlled study showing that visual scripting makes professionals more productive or their code easier to maintain than text. CS1 (adult) evidence exists only for dual-modality.

### Cited Findings
- **Blanchard et al. 2020:** university CS1 students (adults, N = 673) gained from dual-modality instruction on reading and definitional exams but not on the SCS1 (see section 2). — [Blanchard et al. 2020](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/775/2020/01/blanchard-et-al-SIGCSE2020.pdf)
- "Under the Blueprints: Parsing Unreal Engine's Visual Scripting at Scale" (MSR 2025, data and tool showcase) makes Blueprint graphs analyzable at scale and suggests future code-smell detectors for Blueprints. These are tools, not outcome findings. — [MSR 2025](https://2025.msrconf.org/details/msr-2025-data-and-tool-showcase-track/31/Under-the-Blueprints-Parsing-Unreal-Engine-s-Visual-Scripting-at-Scale)
- "Predicting Defective Visual Code Changes in a Multi-Language AAA Video Game Project" builds defect-prediction models from visual-code metrics and compares them with text-based metric models. Industry data. — [Academia](https://www.academia.edu/111115305/Predicting_Defective_Visual_Code_Changes_in_a_Multi_Language_AAA_Video_Game_Project)
- A 2024 Concordia paper (arXiv 2406.05487) studies visual scripting in game engines; I saw only the listing. "The Software Modeling Paradox in Videogames" (to appear at MODELS 2026) compares Unreal Blueprints with Unity Visual Scripting; I could not find its results. — [arXiv 2406.05487](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05487v2); [Zenodo replication package](https://zenodo.org/records/19254652)

### Inferences
- The fact that industry needs Blueprint complexity metrics and "de-spaghetti" talks suggests that large visual graphs develop maintainability problems of their own. It does not show they are worse than text.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous studies of App Inventor with adults, MakeCode in industry, or controlled comparisons of Blueprints against C++ or text for professionals. I did not search the end-user programming literature (spreadsheets, IFTTT) in depth.

## Implications for a blocks-as-view-of-Python game editor

- **Lead with blocks for novices, but don't oversell them.** Expect a small-to-moderate early boost in comprehension, completion and interest (d ≈ 0.6 in the best study; g ≈ 0.25–0.5 in meta-analyses), not lasting superiority. The evidence supports blocks as an *on-ramp*, and the design doc should not claim more. ([Weintrop & Wilensky 2017](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/2017/a3_weintrop_wilensky.pdf), [2019](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Weintrop&Wilensky_2019_C&E.pdf), [Xu et al. 2019](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1218045))
- **The lossless dual view is the best-supported design.** The positive evidence on transfer comes from environments that show the same program as blocks and as text and allow free switching. Learners drift to text and come back to blocks for unfamiliar constructs. Supporting per-construct or per-function switching, rather than a one-way "graduate to text" button, matches observed behavior. ([Weintrop & Holbert 2017](https://ccl.netlogo.org/2017/Blocks.pdf), [Blanchard et al. 2020](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/775/2020/01/blanchard-et-al-SIGCSE2020.pdf), [Kazemitabaar et al. 2022](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Kazemitabaar_et_al_IDC_2022.pdf))
- **Authenticity is a selling point.** The main objection from older or ambitious learners is "not real programming". Showing real Python (with `from sage import world`, real imports and real decorators) in the same editor answers that objection directly.
- **Design against the known bad habits.** Scratch encourages bottom-up tinkering, many tiny scripts, duplication and long scripts, and procedures are rare. Make extracting a function from a block stack a single gesture, show duplicate stacks, and treat smells (dead handlers, duplication, long systems) as editor hints. Children understand smelly programs measurably worse. ([Meerbaum-Salant et al. 2011](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/habits-of-programming-in-scratch/), [Aivaloglou & Hermans 2016](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/how-kids-code-and-how-we-know-an-exploratory-study-on-the-scratch/))
- **Make concurrency and order explicit.** Implicit parallel scripts are a documented source of misconceptions in Scratch. The engine's deterministic, StableId-ordered systems in fixed pipeline phases are an advantage, but only if the block view shows which phase a system runs in and in what order. ([Meerbaum-Salant et al. 2013](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/learning-computer-science-concepts-with-scratch/))
- **Fewer syntax errors is the solid, mechanical benefit.** Frame-based and block editors reduce time spent on syntax and time with non-compiling code. Keep the text view structure-aware as well, in the manner of Stride, so text users get part of this benefit. ([Price et al. 2016](https://isnap.csc.ncsu.edu/home/public/publication/price-2016-icer))
- **Social features correlate with learning; make remixing first-class.** Remixing is associated with concept uptake, and giving feedback is associated with later recognition. But only about 10% of Scratch projects are remixed, and highlighting prominent creators lowers originality. Because projects are small databases (scenes plus scripts), "remix this project" is cheap to build. ([Dasgupta et al. 2016](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=168717), [Hill & Monroy-Hernández 2013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.01295), [CSCW 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.04046))
- **Measure your own users.** Dr. Scratch-style feature counts are a weak proxy for understanding. If the engine collects telemetry (opt-in), it should track modality switching, the use of functions and components, and first-week sharing and feedback, which the literature links to outcomes. ([Bubnič critique](https://press.um.si/index.php/ump/catalog/view/886/1346/4484))
- **For the high ceiling, there is no evidence to lean on.** Nothing shows that visual scripting scales well for professionals, and the Blueprint community is building complexity metrics to manage spaghetti graphs. Treat text as the primary form at scale and blocks as a lens, which is what a lossless view of Python already implies. ([Unreal Fest 2024](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/1986155), [MSR 2025](https://2025.msrconf.org/details/msr-2025-data-and-tool-showcase-track/31/Under-the-Blueprints-Parsing-Unreal-Engine-s-Visual-Scripting-at-Scale))
